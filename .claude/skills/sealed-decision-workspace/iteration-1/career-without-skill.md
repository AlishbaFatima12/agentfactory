## DECISION DOCUMENT

**Decision Context:** Choose between a Series B startup ($210K) and a FAANG company ($150K) by Friday. You have a spouse, two kids under 5, a new $4,200/month mortgage, and no growth path at your current job. This decision is hard because it pits financial upside and career acceleration against family stability during a high-obligation life stage.

---

**RECOMMENDATION:** Take the FAANG offer.

---

**REASONING (282 words):**

The $60K/year pay gap is real but survivable. The stability gap is not.

Your financial profile is load-bearing: $4,200/month mortgage ($50,400/year) on a recently purchased home, two kids under 5 (childcare alone is likely $25-40K/year), and a spouse whose income situation you haven't mentioned — which means I should assume single-income or primary-earner. At $150K FAANG, your after-tax take-home still covers your fixed obligations with margin. At a startup with 18 months of runway, you're betting your family's housing stability on a company that may need to raise again before your kids start kindergarten.

What you're giving up: The startup's $60K premium, likely equity upside, faster title progression, and the energy of a smaller team where your work is more visible. These are real costs. If you were 26 and renting, I'd say take the startup without hesitation.

But you're not. You're in peak financial obligation years. A startup running out of runway means job searching under duress — with a mortgage, with kids, possibly in a down market. FAANG gives you a stable platform to build from: predictable income, strong benefits (healthcare for a family of 4 is worth $15-25K/year), RSU vesting, internal mobility, and a resume line that opens every door if you leave in 2-3 years.

The growth problem you're solving — "no growth path at current job" — is fully solved by FAANG. You get career progression, learning, and brand. The startup solves it too, but adds existential financial risk you don't need to take right now.

This isn't "startup vs. big company" in the abstract. It's "startup vs. big company when you have $50K/year in non-negotiable housing costs and two dependents in diapers."

---

**CONFIDENCE: 72%**

Why this level: The financial obligation profile strongly favors stability, and FAANG solves the stated problem (no growth path) without the downside risk. But I'm not at 85%+ because I don't know your spouse's income, your savings runway, the startup's specific metrics, or your risk tolerance. A dual-income household with 12 months of savings changes this math meaningfully.

---

**MISSING INFORMATION (ranked by decision impact):**

**#1: Your household's financial runway if you lost income for 6 months.** If you have $150K+ in liquid savings and your spouse earns enough to cover the mortgage alone, the startup risk drops dramatically — and my recommendation might flip. **IMPACT: Could reverse the decision.**

**#2: The startup's actual financial health — revenue trajectory, burn rate, likelihood of raising Series C.** "18 months of runway" could mean "growing fast, will raise easily" or "burning cash, may fold." A startup at 2x year-over-year revenue growth with strong unit economics is a very different bet than one burning through capital with no clear path to profitability. **IMPACT: Could change confidence from 72% to either 55% (flip territory) or 85% (locks in FAANG).**

**#3: What equity package the startup is offering and at what valuation.** If they're offering 0.5%+ equity at a reasonable valuation, the expected value calculation shifts — you're not just comparing $210K to $150K, you're comparing $150K + RSUs to $210K + startup equity. The asymmetric upside matters. **IMPACT: Changes the magnitude of what you're giving up, though doesn't change the risk calculus alone.**

---

**REVERSAL TRIGGER:**

I would change my recommendation to "take the startup" if **all three** of these are true: (1) your household has 6+ months of expenses ($40K+) in liquid savings, (2) your spouse's income alone can cover the mortgage payment, and (3) the startup's annual revenue is growing at 80%+ year-over-year with a credible Series C pipeline. Meeting only one or two of these is insufficient — all three must hold to adequately de-risk the startup path for your family situation.

---

**What to do before Friday:** Get answers to gap #1 (a conversation with your spouse tonight) and gap #2 (ask the startup CEO directly: "What's your revenue run rate, growth rate, and Series C timeline?"). If both answers come back strong, revisit. If either is weak, the FAANG offer is the clear call.
