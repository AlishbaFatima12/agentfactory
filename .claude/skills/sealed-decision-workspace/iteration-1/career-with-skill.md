# DECISION DOCUMENT

**Decision Context:** Choose between two job offers by Friday. Offer A is a Series B startup at $210K. Offer B is a FAANG company at $150K. The decision is hard because Offer A pays significantly more but carries runway risk, while Offer B offers stability at lower stated comp. Stakes are high: new $4,200/month mortgage, spouse, two children under 5. Cost of deciding wrong (startup folds, family under financial stress) is materially higher than the cost of leaving money on the table.

---

**RECOMMENDATION:** Accept the FAANG offer and negotiate the base salary upward using the $210K competing offer as leverage.

---

**REASONING (280 words):**

The core tension here is not $210K vs. $150K — it is asymmetric downside risk. With a $4,200 mortgage and two kids under 5, a startup that runs out of runway in 18 months does not just cost you a job. It costs you 3-6 months of emergency burn while job searching, likely during the worst possible hiring window (startups tend to die in clusters during downturns), with dependents who cannot absorb instability.

The FAANG offer likely understates total compensation. RSUs, annual bonus, 401(k) match, and family health/dental benefits at FAANG companies routinely add $50-100K in annual value. The real gap may be narrow or nonexistent — and the FAANG comp is liquid and predictable, while startup equity above $210K base is illiquid and contingent on outcomes you cannot control.

You said your current job has no growth path. The FAANG solves that problem completely — structured levels, internal mobility, and a resume line that opens every future door, including future startup roles when your kids are older and your mortgage is seasoned.

What you give up: the adrenaline and potential outsized equity return of the startup, the likely smaller team where you shape more, and the immediate cash-flow boost. These are real costs. But they are costs you can afford to pay now and recover later. The reverse — taking the startup, having it fold in month 14 with two toddlers and a new mortgage — is a hole that takes years to climb out of.

You are not choosing between adventure and boredom. You are choosing sequencing. The FAANG offer lets you build stability now and take asymmetric risks later from a position of strength.

---

**CONFIDENCE: 72%**

Why this level: The family financial obligations and runway risk create a clear structural argument for stability. I'm not higher because the actual total comp comparison is unknown (FAANG RSUs could make this obvious, or could disappoint), and because individual startup risk tolerance varies — someone with a working spouse earning $150K+ faces a very different calculus than a single-income household.

---

**MISSING INFORMATION (ranked by decision impact):**

**#1: FAANG total compensation (RSUs, bonus, benefits value)** — Impact: If FAANG total comp is $220K+, the decision becomes obvious and confidence rises to 85%+. If total comp is truly just $150K with minimal equity, the financial sacrifice is real and confidence drops to 60%.

**#2: Spouse's employment income and stability** — Impact: If spouse earns $100K+ with stable employment, the family can absorb a startup failure, which would drop my confidence to 55% and make the startup a legitimate choice. If single-income household, confidence rises to 85%.

**#3: Startup's specific burn rate, revenue trajectory, and Series C prospects** — Impact: If the startup is at $8M ARR growing 3x with clear Series C interest, the "18 months runway" is a formality and the risk profile changes entirely. If pre-revenue burning $2M/month, the 18-month clock is very real.

---

**REVERSAL TRIGGER:**

I would change my recommendation to the startup if ALL THREE of the following are true: (1) FAANG total comp confirms below $190K all-in, (2) spouse has stable income above $100K/year, AND (3) the startup has more than $5M ARR with documented Series C investor interest. Any two of three would move me to 50/50. All three would flip the recommendation.
