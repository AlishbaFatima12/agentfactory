# The Feedback Loop

James spread all three drafts across his kitchen table. Draft 1 on the left, scribbled on lined paper the night he started the chapter. The merged map in the middle, covered in annotations where he had marked which connections came from him, which from Claude, which from ChatGPT. Draft 3 on the right, revised after the variable shift, with green and red ink marking what changed and what survived.

He sat back and looked at them together for the first time.

"There's a lot of paper," he said.

Emma pulled up a chair across from him. She picked up Draft 1 and held it at arm's length, squinting. "What do you see?"

"A mess."

"More specific."

James leaned forward. "Okay. In Draft 1, I have five domains. Employees, customers, competitors, regulators, internal knowledge. But the connections are all... one-directional. Everything flows out from the decision. Employees lose jobs. Customers lose personal service. Competitors gain an advantage." He paused. "It reads like a list with arrows drawn on it."

"And Draft 3?"

He picked up the right-hand page. "Draft 3 has loops. The customer churn feeds back into revenue, which feeds back into the cost savings that started the whole thing. The regulator response changes based on public sentiment, which changes based on how the bank handles the transition." He traced a circle with his finger. "It's not a list anymore. It's a... web."

"So what changed?" Emma asked. "Did you learn new facts between Draft 1 and Draft 3?"

James thought about it. "Not really. The bank scenario didn't change. Well, except for the variable shift. But even before that, the merged map had connections I could have drawn myself. I just... didn't think to."

"That's the interesting part." Emma set down Draft 1. "The facts were always available to you. What changed was how you approached them."

## The Thinking-Change Test

James frowned. He picked up a pen and started writing on a blank piece of paper, then stopped. "Wait. Are you saying that adding a missing domain to my map isn't actually growth? Because I definitely missed the regulatory feedback loop in Draft 1 and caught it in Draft 3."

"Catching a specific gap is a content change," Emma said. "You added a connection you were missing. That matters. But it's not the same as a thinking change."

"What's the difference?"

Emma held up Draft 1 again. "In this map, how did you decide when to stop tracing effects?"

James looked at it. "I think I stopped when I ran out of obvious things to write. Each domain got two or three effects and then I moved on."

"And in Draft 3?"

"I stopped when the loops closed. I kept asking 'and then what happens?' until the chain circled back to something I had already written." He stared at the two drafts. "That's... a different stopping rule."

"That's a thinking change," Emma said. "Your stopping criterion shifted from 'I ran out of ideas' to 'the causal chain reached a natural closure point.' That shift will transfer to every systems analysis you do from now on. The specific regulatory feedback loop you missed in Draft 1 will not."

James wrote on his blank page: **Thinking change: my stopping rule shifted from exhaustion to closure.**

This is what the **Thinking-Change Test** reveals. When you compare your early work to your late work, you are looking for changes in *how you think*, not just changes in *what you produced*. A content change improves one map. A thinking change improves every map you will ever draw.

:::tip The Thinking-Change Test
Compare your Draft 1 and Draft 3 side by side. For every difference you spot, ask: "Would this change help me on a completely different scenario?" If yes, it is a thinking change. If it only applies to this specific scenario, it is a content change. Both matter, but thinking changes compound.
:::

## Tracing Where Growth Came From

Emma picked up her coffee. "You have four exercises' worth of evidence. Draft 1, the AI comparison, the variable shift, the peer defence. That's a paper trail. Use it."

"Use it for what?"

"To figure out what actually changed your thinking. Not all feedback sources are equal."

James flipped through his notes. The AI comparison from Exercise 2 had identified six connections he missed. The variable shift in Exercise 3 had forced him to rebuild half his map. His peer had challenged one of his feedback loops as implausible.

"The variable shift hurt the most," he admitted. "I had to throw out connections I was proud of. Three of my feedback loops just... stopped working when the context changed."

"Why did they stop working?"

James looked at his change log from Exercise 3. "Because I had assumed the customer base was urban and tech-literate. When the variable shift changed it to 60% unbanked, my whole 'customers switch to competitors' chain collapsed. Unbanked customers don't have alternatives to switch to."

"So what did the variable shift teach you that the AI comparison didn't?"

He thought for a long moment. "The AI comparison showed me connections I missed. That's useful but it's... additive. The variable shift showed me that connections I was confident about were conditional. They only worked under assumptions I hadn't even noticed I was making." He picked up his pen again. "That's different. The AI gave me more. The variable shift made me doubt what I already had."

Emma almost smiled. "Which one is harder?"

"Doubting what you already have." James wrote: **Variable shift taught me that my 'confident' connections had hidden assumptions. AI taught me I was incomplete. The peer taught me I couldn't explain my own reasoning under pressure.**

This is **Growth Source Mapping**: the practice of tracing each thinking change back to the feedback source that caused it. Not all sources produce the same type of growth.

| Feedback Source | What It Reveals | Growth Type |
|---|---|---|
| AI comparison (Exercise 2) | Gaps in breadth: connections and domains you did not think of | Additive: you learn to cast a wider net |
| Variable shift (Exercise 3) | Hidden assumptions: connections that only hold under specific conditions | Corrective: you learn to test your own assumptions |
| Peer cross-examination (Exercise 4) | Explanatory gaps: places where your understanding is shallower than your map suggests | Depth: you learn to distinguish drawing a connection from understanding it |

Each type of growth requires a different kind of practice to sustain. Additive growth comes from exposure to more perspectives. Corrective growth comes from stress-testing your own work. Depth growth comes from being forced to explain your reasoning aloud.

## The Personal Improvement Map

Emma stood up. "I need to make a call. Twenty minutes." She picked up her coffee and walked toward the hallway, then turned back. "By the time I get back, I want to see your improvement map. Not a list of things you got wrong. A map of how you plan to get better." She left.

James stared at the blank paper. An improvement map. Not a list.

He started with what he knew. His biggest thinking change: the stopping rule. His biggest weakness: hidden assumptions. His peer had found a gap he should have caught. The AI had identified a whole domain he had underweighted.

He wrote down three categories. Things that changed. Things that should have changed but did not. Things he wanted to change next.

Under "things that changed," he wrote: *I stopped listing effects and started tracing loops. I started asking "under what conditions?" instead of treating every connection as universal. I got better at explaining my reasoning because the peer defence forced me to.*

Under "things that should have changed but did not," he thought harder. He looked at the AI feedback across all four exercises. There was a pattern. Every time the AI evaluated his map, it flagged the same type of gap: he consistently underweighted institutional actors. Regulators, boards, industry bodies. His business operations background made him think in terms of direct stakeholders (employees, customers, suppliers) and he kept treating institutional actors as background constraints instead of dynamic participants in the system.

He wrote: *I treat regulators and institutional actors as static walls instead of active players with their own feedback loops. This is my old operations mindset: I managed the team and the suppliers, and I treated compliance as a checkbox. In systems thinking, compliance is a living actor that responds to what the system does.*

Under "things I wanted to change next," he wrote something specific: *Next time I build a cascade map, I will start with institutional actors first. Not employees or customers. If I can force myself to put regulators and industry bodies at the center for at least the first pass, I will find the feedback loops I keep missing.*

He looked at what he had written. It was concrete. It was testable. If he did Chapter 4 and still underweighted institutional actors, he would know his improvement plan had failed.

When Emma came back, she read it standing up. She set down her coffee and tapped the third section.

"This is specific," she said.

"You told me not to make a list."

"I told you to make a map. There's a difference." She sat down. "A list says 'get better at finding feedback loops.' A map says 'I underweight institutional actors because of my operations background, so I will start my next analysis with institutional actors first, and I will check whether that changes the feedback loops I find.' The second version can fail. The first one cannot."

"How can it not fail?"

"Because it's too vague to test. If your goal is 'get better at systems thinking,' you can always tell yourself you improved a little. If your goal is 'start with institutional actors and check whether that changes my feedback loops,' you will know in thirty minutes whether it worked."

James looked at his improvement map. "So the improvement map is just... another feedback loop. I look at my work, identify what changed, figure out what should change next, and then test it."

"Now you're seeing it."

"The whole chapter was a feedback loop."

Emma picked up Draft 1 and set it next to the improvement map. "Draft 1 was your input. Four exercises ran you through amplifying and dampening cycles. Peer feedback, AI feedback, variable shifts. And this improvement map is your output, which feeds back into how you approach the next system you analyze."

James traced the circle with his finger. Input, process, output, and the output becomes the next input. The same structure he had mapped for the bank's customers and the hospital's patients and the city's commuters. Applied to himself.

"It's the same pattern," he said quietly.

"It usually is."

This is the **Personal Improvement Map**: a structured self-evaluation tool that transforms backward reflection into forward practice.

### How to Build Your Improvement Map

| Section | Question to Answer | Evidence to Use |
|---|---|---|
| **What changed** | Which thinking habits shifted between Draft 1 and Draft 3? | Side-by-side comparison of maps, your change logs |
| **What should have changed** | What patterns appear in the feedback you received across all four exercises? | AI evaluations, peer feedback, your own reflections |
| **What to change next** | What is one specific, testable practice you will try in your next analysis? | The weakest pattern identified in section 2 |

The test for a good improvement map: can it fail? If your plan is specific enough that you could look at your next piece of work and determine whether the plan succeeded or not, it is a real improvement map. If it is too vague to fail, it is a wish.

### What This Teaches You

You learn that the same feedback loop structure you mapped for banks, hospitals, and cities applies to your own learning. Self-evaluation is not a fuzzy exercise in "reflection." It is a systems thinking skill: identify inputs (your early work), trace the process (what feedback changed your approach), evaluate the output (your improved work), and design the next iteration (your improvement plan). The students who build this habit will improve faster across every chapter that follows, because they are not just learning content. They are tuning the system that learns.

:::note Looking Ahead
You have now completed the **Systems Thinking Portfolio**: five exercises that took you from a first-draft cascade map to a personal improvement plan. In Chapter 4, you will apply this same structured thinking to a new domain. Your improvement map is not just a deliverable for this chapter; it is your preparation for the next one.
:::
