# The Feedback Loop

James spread all three maps across Emma's desk. Draft 1 on the left, crumpled at the edges where he had erased and redrawn arrows. Draft 2 in the middle, cleaner, with two colors of ink where he had merged AI suggestions. Draft 3 on the right, covered in green and red marks from the variable shift.

"There," he said. "Done. Four exercises, three drafts, one portfolio."

Emma glanced at the maps but did not pick any of them up. "What changed?"

"Everything. The first one has maybe twelve connections. The third one has thirty."

"So your thinking improved because your map got bigger?"

James paused. He had been about to say yes. "I mean, it's more complete. More domains, more effects, more feedback loops."

"More effects." Emma pulled Draft 1 toward her and pointed at a connection between employees and customers. "This arrow. What mechanism did you write?"

James leaned over. His own handwriting stared back at him: *Fewer employees leads to worse customer experience.* No explanation. No mechanism. Just a claim.

"Now look at Draft 3."

He found the same connection. This time it read: *Replacing loan officers removes the personal relationships that drive loan renewals for unbanked customers, reducing repeat business by removing the trust mechanism, not just the labor.*

"Okay," James said slowly. "That's not just more words."

"What is it?"

He thought about it. When he drew Draft 1, he had written the first thing that came to mind. Fewer people, worse service. Obvious. But after comparing his map against two AI tools and then rebuilding it when the variable shifted, he had stopped writing obvious connections and started asking himself: *why* does this effect follow from this cause? What is the actual mechanism?

"It's a different kind of thinking," he said. "Draft 1 was listing things. Draft 3 was explaining things."

"Name it."

"What?"

"If that change has no name, you will lose it by next week. You will go back to listing." Emma tapped the desk. "Name the move you made."

## The Growth Delta

James sat with that for a moment. He could feel the difference between his first draft and his third. It was not just more items on the map. It was the quality of each connection. But he had never been asked to name a thinking change before. You learned things, sure, but nobody asked you to label the shift in *how* you thought.

"The mechanism check," he said. "Every arrow gets a mechanism. Not just 'A causes B' but 'A causes B because of this specific thing that connects them.'"

Emma almost nodded. "That's your **Growth Delta**: the specific, identifiable difference between how you thought at Draft 1 and how you think now. It is not a feeling. It is not 'I got better.' It is a named, testable move."

"Wait, so basically, the Growth Delta is like a process improvement metric?" James leaned back. "At my old company, we never said 'the warehouse got better.' We said 'we reduced pick-to-ship time by forty minutes because we reorganized the staging area.' The improvement had a name and a cause."

"Same principle. Vague improvement is not improvement. If you cannot name it, you cannot repeat it."

James looked at his three maps again with different eyes. The Growth Delta was not about the maps getting bigger. It was about the arrows getting stronger.

But something bothered him.

"Here's what I don't get," he said. "Half the improvements in Draft 2 came from AI. I compared my map against Claude and ChatGPT, found gaps, merged them in. So how much of the Growth Delta is actually mine?"

Emma pulled Draft 2 toward her. "Show me a connection that AI suggested."

James pointed. "This one. Competitors poaching displaced loan officers. Claude found that. I missed it completely."

"And now, without looking at your notes, explain the mechanism."

"If the bank fires experienced loan officers, competitors hire them because those officers have existing relationships with customers. The customers follow the officers because the trust was with the person, not the institution. So the bank loses customers to competitors who hired its own former employees." He paused. "That's a feedback loop, actually. The cost savings from firing officers leads to customer loss from competitors poaching, which reduces revenue, which increases pressure for more cost savings."

"Did Claude describe that feedback loop?"

James checked his notes. "No. Claude listed the poaching effect. I connected it to the feedback loop when I was building Draft 3."

"So who owns the Growth Delta?"

**The Ownership Test**: if you can explain a connection's mechanism from memory, trace its feedback loops, and adapt it when conditions change, you own it. It does not matter who first suggested it. What matters is whether the understanding lives in your head or only in your notes.

James thought about his time managing supplier relationships. "That's like onboarding a new vendor," he said. "Someone else recommends them, but if I cannot explain to my boss why we chose them and what happens when their lead time changes, I do not actually understand the supply chain. I just copied someone's suggestion."

"And if you can explain it?"

"Then it is mine. Even though I did not discover it first."

### Surface Improvement vs. Structural Improvement

Emma picked up a pen and drew two columns on a blank sheet.

"Your maps changed in two ways. Count the connections in Draft 1."

James counted. "Fourteen."

"Draft 3?"

"Thirty-one."

"That is surface improvement: **more nodes, more arrows, more stuff on the page.** Now count the connections that have a written mechanism."

James counted more carefully. Draft 1: two connections had anything resembling a mechanism. Draft 3: twenty-three.

"That is structural improvement: **stronger connections, clearer mechanisms, genuine feedback loops.** A map with fourteen connections and fourteen mechanisms beats a map with thirty-one connections and zero mechanisms."

"So when I said 'my map got bigger' and you didn't look impressed..."

"Bigger is easy. Deeper is hard. More is not the same as better." She pushed the paper toward him. "For your reflection, I need you to separate the two. Which changes between your drafts were surface, and which were structural?"

James frowned. "But some of my surface improvements led to structural ones. Adding the competitors domain was surface: more stuff on the map. But once it was there, I found the feedback loop with poached employees. That was structural."

Emma set down her pen. She had not expected that.

"That's actually a really good observation," she said. "I usually describe surface and structural as separate categories. But you are right that surface changes sometimes create the conditions for structural ones. I have been simplifying that." She paused. "I will have to think about whether the categories still hold when one leads to the other."

James blinked. He was not used to Emma revising her own framework mid-conversation.

"Write that down in your reflection," she said. "When surface improvement enables structural improvement, which category does it belong to? That is worth more than a clean answer."

## Your Turn: Finding Your Growth Delta

Emma stood up. "I am going to check on something. When I get back, I want you to have three things written down." She held up three fingers.

"One: your Growth Delta. Named. One sentence. No vague words like 'better' or 'improved.'

Two: three specific connections from your maps, classified as surface or structural improvement. For each one, explain why you classified it that way.

Three: one thinking move you made in this chapter that you did not make in Chapter 2. Something you do now that you were not doing two weeks ago."

She walked out.

James stared at his three maps. The Growth Delta was clear: the mechanism check. He could name that one. But the second task was harder. He started comparing Draft 1 and Draft 3, arrow by arrow.

The first one was easy. In Draft 1, he had written "regulators respond." In Draft 3, he had written "regulators investigate discriminatory lending patterns because AI systems trained on historical data reproduce the same biases that human officers had, but at scale and without the case-by-case discretion that allowed exceptions." That was structural. The connection did not just exist; it had a chain of reasoning.

The second one was trickier. He had added a connection between internal knowledge loss and training costs. Was that surface or structural? It was a new node. More stuff on the map. But the mechanism was precise: when experienced officers leave, the bank loses tacit knowledge about local market conditions that cannot be extracted from data alone, increasing the cost of training AI systems that need exactly that kind of context. Surface addition, structural mechanism.

The third one tripped him up. A connection between customer trust and competitor strategy. He had written it, but when he tried to explain the mechanism from memory, he could not. He had copied it from Claude's analysis without fully processing it. He knew it was there. He did not know *why* it was there.

That was the Ownership Test in action. He could not explain it, so he did not own it.

He wrote that down too.

When Emma came back, he had his three answers. She read them without expression.

"The one you could not explain," she said. "What are you going to do about it?"

"Go back and figure out the mechanism. Or remove it from my map."

"Why not leave it as is? It is probably correct."

"Because if the variable shifts again and I do not understand that connection, I will not know how to adapt it. I will just stare at it."

Emma let that sit. "That is the most important sentence you have said all chapter."

### The Thinking Pattern

There was one more thing James noticed as he compared his three drafts. The connections he missed in every single draft, even Draft 3, followed a pattern.

"I keep missing the regulatory effects," he said. "In every draft, the regulator domain has the fewest connections and the weakest mechanisms. It is always the last domain I fill in, and I always run out of energy before I get to it."

"That is your **thinking pattern**: the systematic shape of what you reliably see and what you consistently miss."

"Like a blind spot?"

"More than a blind spot. A blind spot is one thing you miss. A thinking pattern is the *shape* of your analysis. You see employee effects first because you managed teams. You see process effects because you optimized workflows. You miss regulatory effects because you never worked in compliance."

James thought about it. "At my old company, I used to get blindsided by compliance audits. I would design a new process and forget about the reporting requirements until legal called me."

"Same pattern. Different domain."

"So my thinking pattern followed me from operations into systems thinking."

"Thinking patterns always follow you. The question is whether you can see them."

**A thinking pattern** is not a flaw to fix but a shape to know. The connections you find easily reveal your strengths. The connections you consistently miss reveal where you need to deliberately look harder, or ask someone with a different pattern to check your work.

### What You Do

Place your three cascade maps side by side. Compare them systematically, not just to admire the progress, but to identify the specific thinking moves that produced improvement. You will distinguish surface improvement (more nodes) from structural improvement (better connections) and name your Growth Delta: the single most important change in how you think, not what you know.

### Step 1: The Draft Comparison

For each pair of drafts (Draft 1 to Draft 2, and Draft 2 to Draft 3), identify:

- **Connections added** (new arrows that did not exist before)
- **Connections strengthened** (same arrow, but now with a mechanism or a tighter chain)
- **Connections removed or replaced** (arrows you deleted because they were wrong or vague)
- **Feedback loops** that appeared for the first time

For each change, classify it: **Surface** (more stuff) or **Structural** (deeper understanding).

### Step 2: Name Your Growth Delta

Write one sentence that names the most important change in your thinking process across this chapter. Rules:

- No vague words: not "better," "improved," "deeper," or "more sophisticated"
- Must be a **thinking move**, not a content addition: "I now check every arrow for a mechanism" qualifies. "I added more domains" does not
- Must be **testable**: someone could watch you build a cascade map and verify whether you are doing this move

### Step 3: Identify Your Thinking Pattern

Review all the feedback you received across the chapter: AI feedback from Exercises 1-3, peer feedback from Exercise 4, and your own observations. Identify:

- **What you reliably find**: the types of connections, domains, or effects you consistently identify across all three drafts
- **What you consistently miss**: the types you added only after AI or peer feedback, or the types still missing in Draft 3
- **Why the pattern exists**: connect it to your background, experience, or habits

<summary>Growth Delta Examples (click to expand)</summary>

These are examples of well-formed and poorly-formed Growth Delta statements:

**Well-formed (specific, testable, describes a thinking move):**
- "I now trace every arrow through at least one intermediary step before accepting it as a direct connection."
- "I check each domain for at least one feedback loop before moving to the next domain."
- "I ask 'what is the mechanism?' for every causal claim, including my own."

**Poorly-formed (vague, describes content, not testable):**
- "I got better at systems thinking." (Better how? Not testable.)
- "I added more domains to my map." (Content addition, not a thinking move.)
- "I think more deeply now." (What does 'deeply' mean? Not observable.)
- "My maps improved a lot." (Improved in what specific way?)

### What This Teaches You

James picked up his Draft 1 map one more time. The crumpled paper with its twelve weak connections. He thought about what Emma had said: *if you cannot name the change, you cannot repeat it.*

He could name it now. The mechanism check. Every arrow gets a reason. And he knew his thinking pattern: strong on operations and people effects, weak on regulatory and institutional dynamics. Not a flaw to fix by tomorrow. A shape to know, so he could deliberately look into the gaps instead of waiting for someone else to find them.

The chapter had been about systems thinking. But this last exercise was about something else: the ability to watch your own mind at work and describe what it does. Not what you learned. What changed in *how* you learn.

That was the feedback loop the chapter was named for. Not the loops on the cascade map. The loop between doing the work, evaluating the work, and changing how you do the work next time.

"One more question," James said.

Emma looked up.

"Chapter 2 taught me to spot errors in AI output. This chapter taught me to trace systems. In both chapters, the last exercise asked me to look back at how my thinking changed." He drummed his fingers on the desk. "Is that going to happen every chapter?"

"What do you think?"

"I think that is a pattern."

"Good. Name it."

He sat with it. "The reflection loop. Every chapter's last exercise turns the skill back on itself."

Emma picked up her coffee. "Now you are thinking in systems."
