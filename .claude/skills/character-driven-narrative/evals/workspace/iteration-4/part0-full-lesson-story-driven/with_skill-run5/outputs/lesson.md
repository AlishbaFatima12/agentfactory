# The Feedback Loop

James spread his four drafts across the kitchen table. Draft 1, drawn on a Tuesday evening with a pen that kept smudging, looked like a child's treasure map compared to Draft 3. The arrows were thicker in the later version, the labels were longer, and there were three feedback loops he hadn't imagined existing when he started.

He should have felt proud. He didn't.

"Something's bothering you," Emma said. She was leaning against the doorframe with a mug of tea, watching him rearrange the pages.

"I can see that Draft 3 is better," James said. "But I can't tell you _why_ it's better. Not in a way that would help me do it again."

Emma set the mug down and pulled out a chair. "That's the most important thing you've said all chapter."

---

## The Problem with "I Got Better"

James pointed at Draft 1's feedback loop for the bank scenario. "Look at this one. 'Cost savings lead to worse service lead to customer churn lead to less revenue.' That's what I wrote on day one."

"And Draft 3?"

He slid the later version next to it. "Now it says: 'Replacing loan officers eliminates relationship-based credit assessment. Customers who relied on personal advocacy for non-standard loan applications lose access. Default rates among small business borrowers increase because automated systems reject applications that experienced officers would have approved on judgment. The bank tightens lending criteria further in response to rising defaults, which reduces the customer base, which reduces revenue below the savings from automation.'"

Emma nodded slowly. "So what changed?"

"It's longer."

"That's an observation. Not an explanation."

James stared at the two versions. He thought about his old job in operations, where his quarterly performance reviews always said things like "improved stakeholder management" without ever explaining what that meant. He could never tell if he was actually getting better or if his manager was just running out of ways to say "adequate."

"Okay, wait," he said. "The first version names _what_ happens. The second version names _why_ it happens. The mechanism."

Emma almost smiled. "Now you're evaluating, not just comparing."

---

## The Growth Evidence Taxonomy

"There's a name for what you just did," Emma said. She pulled a napkin toward her and drew four boxes.

"You always draw on things," James muttered.

"Napkins are cheaper than whiteboards." She wrote a label in each box:

:::note The Growth Evidence Taxonomy
When evaluating how your systems thinking improved, look for four distinct types of growth. Each one is a different kind of improvement.

| Growth Type                      | What It Means                                                                    | Evidence Looks Like                                                                                                                      |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Depth**                        | Your causal chains got longer and more mechanistic                               | "A leads to B" became "A leads to B because X, which triggers C"                                                                         |
| **Breadth**                      | You covered more domains or found cross-domain connections                       | Draft 1 missed regulators entirely; Draft 3 traces two regulatory feedback loops                                                         |
| **Mechanism Quality**            | You explained WHY each connection exists, not just THAT it exists                | "Customer churn increases" became "Customers who relied on personal advocacy lose access to non-standard lending"                        |
| **Feedback Loop Sophistication** | Your loops got tighter: specific triggers, named thresholds, plausible timelines | "Costs go up" became "Default rates among small business borrowers cross the 8% threshold that triggers automated portfolio rebalancing" |

:::

"So these are four separate things," James said. He looked at his drafts again. "And I could have improved on one without improving on the others."

"Exactly. You could add six new domains and still have shallow mechanisms in every one of them."

"That's like our old performance reviews," James said, leaning back. "My manager would say I had 'broader stakeholder engagement' when all I'd done was CC more people on emails. Breadth without depth."

Emma pointed at him. "That analogy. Keep it."

"Why?"

"Because next time someone tells you their analysis 'got better,' you'll ask: better on which dimension? That question is worth more than the answer."

---

James pulled out a blank sheet and started listing what had changed between his drafts. He wrote quickly, slotting each improvement into one of the four categories. Depth: his causal chains had grown from two steps to four or five. Breadth: he'd added connections between employees and regulators that he'd completely missed in Draft 1. Mechanism Quality: he'd stopped writing "leads to" and started writing "because."

He felt a flicker of satisfaction. It was working. He could see the improvement now, not as a vague feeling, but as labeled evidence.

Then Emma said, "Now do the hard part."

"That wasn't the hard part?"

"That was the easy part. You just listed where you got better." She tapped the napkin. "Where did you stay the same?"

---

## The Persistent Blind Spot

James stared at her. "You want me to find what I'm _still_ bad at."

"I want you to find what you're _consistently_ bad at. One-time misses are noise. Patterns are signal."

"Hang on," James said. He was thinking about something. At his old company, the warehouse team had the same supplier delivery problem every quarter. They'd fix it, it would recur, they'd fix it differently, it would recur again. His operations director eventually stopped asking "what went wrong this time" and started asking "what keeps going wrong?" That question changed everything. They found the real bottleneck: the approval workflow, not the suppliers.

"That's actually what feedback loops do," James said, sitting up. "In the chapter, I mean. The whole thing is a feedback loop."

Emma said nothing. She waited.

"Draft 1 was my first pass. AI found gaps. I revised. The variable shift broke some of my connections, so I revised again. My peer found more gaps. Each round fed back into the next draft." He paused. "But some gaps kept showing up. That means the problem isn't in any single draft. It's in how I think."

"Go on."

James pulled all four drafts into a line. He checked each AI feedback response, his peer's critique, and his own change logs. He was looking for a pattern.

Five minutes later, he found it.

"I keep missing the internal knowledge domain," he said. "In Draft 1, I didn't mention it at all. In Draft 2, AI told me about it, so I added it. In Draft 3, the variable shift should have changed my internal knowledge connections, but I barely touched them. And my peer didn't catch it because she missed that domain too."

"What does that tell you?"

James thought about it. "It's not that I forgot. It's that I don't naturally think about organizational knowledge as a system that gets affected by decisions. I think about people, money, customers, and rules. But the knowledge that lives inside an organization, how it moves, who holds it, how it degrades when people leave. That's invisible to me."

Emma picked up her tea. "That's worth more than every improvement you listed."

"How?"

"Because now you have a practice target. Not 'get better at systems thinking.' That's useless. Your target is: 'In every cascade map I draw from now on, I will start with the internal knowledge domain and trace effects through it first, before moving to the domains I find natural.'"

James wrote it down.

## Your Self-Evaluation

**Layers Used:** Layer 6 (Iterative Drafts)

:::note Building On the Full Chapter
This exercise uses every artifact you produced in Lessons 1-4: your original cascade map (Draft 1), the merged map (Draft 2), the revised map after variable shift (Draft 3), your peer critique notes, all AI feedback, and your change logs. Gather them before you start.
:::

### What You Do

Place your Draft 1 and Draft 3 side by side. Using the Growth Evidence Taxonomy, identify at least three specific improvements in your systems thinking with evidence from the maps themselves. Then identify at least one persistent blind spot: a type of gap that recurred across multiple drafts or review rounds, suggesting a pattern in your thinking rather than a one-time miss.

### Step 1: The Side-by-Side Comparison

Place Draft 1 (your original cascade map, before any AI or peer input) next to Draft 3 (your revised map after the variable shift). For each of the four growth dimensions, find concrete evidence:

| Growth Dimension                 | Draft 1 Evidence                           | Draft 3 Evidence                       | What Changed                                   |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **Depth**                        | Quote a specific causal chain from Draft 1 | Quote the same chain from Draft 3      | How many more steps? Are mechanisms named?     |
| **Breadth**                      | List domains covered in Draft 1            | List domains covered in Draft 3        | What domains or cross-domain links were added? |
| **Mechanism Quality**            | Quote a "leads to" connection from Draft 1 | Quote the same connection from Draft 3 | Did "leads to" become "because"?               |
| **Feedback Loop Sophistication** | Quote a feedback loop from Draft 1         | Quote the same loop from Draft 3       | Are triggers, thresholds, or timelines named?  |

### Step 2: The Blind Spot Audit

Review all feedback you received across the chapter:

- AI feedback from Lesson 1 (cascade map evaluation)
- AI feedback from Lesson 2 (human vs. AI comparison)
- AI feedback from Lesson 3 (variable shift evaluation)
- Peer feedback from Lesson 4 (cross-examination)

Look for gaps that appeared in **more than one round**. A gap that AI flagged in Lesson 1 and your peer flagged again in Lesson 4 is a persistent blind spot. A gap that appeared once and never again is a one-time miss.

Write down:

- **The blind spot**: What type of connection or domain do you consistently underweight?
- **The evidence**: Which feedback rounds flagged it?
- **The practice target**: One specific, actionable change to your process that addresses this blind spot in future analyses

### Try With AI

Use these prompts in Claude or your preferred AI assistant.

**Prompt 1: Validate Your Self-Assessment**

```
I completed a chapter on systems thinking where I drew cascade maps
across 4 drafts. I identified these improvements in my thinking:

[Paste your 3+ labeled improvements with evidence]

And this persistent blind spot:

[Paste your blind spot with evidence]

Please evaluate:
(1) Are my claimed improvements supported by the evidence I provided,
or am I overstating my growth?
(2) Is my blind spot a genuine pattern, or could it be a coincidence?
(3) What is one improvement I may have made that I did NOT notice?
```

**What you are learning:** How to use AI as a calibration tool for self-assessment. Your self-evaluation may be too generous or too harsh. AI can check whether your evidence actually supports your claims.

**Prompt 2: Compare Your Blind Spot to Common Patterns**

```
In systems thinking analysis, I consistently underweight [your blind
spot domain or connection type]. I have evidence from 4 rounds of
feedback that this is a pattern, not a one-time miss.

Please:
(1) Is this a common blind spot in systems thinking? What type of
thinker typically misses this?
(2) What specific practice exercise could I do to strengthen this
area?
(3) Give me a 5-minute warm-up I could do before any future systems
analysis to make sure I don't skip this domain.
```

**What you are learning:** How to turn a self-identified weakness into a deliberate practice plan. Knowing your blind spot is step one. Having a routine that compensates for it is step two.

**Prompt 3: Predict Your Next Blind Spot**

```
Here is my growth trajectory across a systems thinking chapter:

Draft 1 weaknesses: [list from AI feedback on Lesson 1]
Draft 3 remaining weaknesses: [list from AI feedback on Lesson 3]
Peer-identified gaps: [list from Lesson 4]
My identified blind spot: [your blind spot]

Based on this trajectory, what blind spot am I LIKELY to encounter
next when I face a more complex scenario with 7+ domains? What
should I watch for?
```

**What you are learning:** How to anticipate future weaknesses before they appear. The best self-evaluators do not just look backward at what went wrong. They use patterns in past performance to predict where they will struggle next.

Emma came back twenty minutes later. James had the growth map spread across the table: three labeled improvements with quoted evidence, one blind spot circled in red, and a practice target written in block letters at the bottom.

"How does it look?" he asked.

She read it standing. She didn't sit down. When she got to the blind spot section, she stopped.

"Internal knowledge domain," she read. "Flagged by AI in Lesson 1. Barely addressed in Draft 3. Missed by peer in Lesson 4." She looked at him. "Why do you think your peer missed it too?"

James hadn't considered that. "Maybe because it's not obvious? People, money, and rules are concrete. Organizational knowledge is... abstract. It's harder to see."

"And what does that suggest about your blind spot?"

"That it's not just mine. It might be a common one." He frowned. "But that doesn't make it less of a problem. If anything, it means the gap is less likely to get caught by anyone reviewing my work."

Emma set the growth map down. "You know what you just did?"

"What?"

"You applied systems thinking to your own learning process. Your blind spot is a node. The fact that peers share it is a feedback loop: if everyone misses it, no one catches it, so no one fixes it, so everyone keeps missing it." She paused. "That's an amplifying loop."

James sat with that for a moment. He thought about the quarterly supplier problem at his old company. The warehouse team kept missing the real bottleneck because everyone on the team had the same assumption about where the problem lived. It took someone from a different department to see it.

"So the fix isn't just 'remember to check internal knowledge,'" he said. "The fix is to deliberately seek reviewers who think differently than I do. Someone who naturally starts with organizational knowledge instead of people and money."

Emma picked up her tea. "Now you're thinking in systems."

### What This Teaches You

You learn that improvement without self-awareness is accidental. The Growth Evidence Taxonomy gives you a vocabulary for describing _how_ your thinking changed, not just _that_ it changed. The persistent blind spot audit turns reflection from a feel-good exercise into a diagnostic tool. And the practice target turns diagnosis into action: one specific change to your process that addresses a real, evidence-based weakness.

The chapter's own structure is a feedback loop. Draft 1 fed into AI comparison, which fed into the variable shift, which fed into peer review, which fed into this self-evaluation. Each round revealed gaps the previous round missed. That same structure, iterating through multiple perspectives and feeding each result into the next revision, is the core of systems thinking itself.
