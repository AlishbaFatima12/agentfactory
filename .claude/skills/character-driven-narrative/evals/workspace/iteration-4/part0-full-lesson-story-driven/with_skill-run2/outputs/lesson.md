# The Feedback Loop

James spread four sheets of paper across the table. Draft 1, the cascade map he had drawn alone on the first day. Draft 2, the merged version with Claude's and ChatGPT's additions marked in blue. Draft 3, the revised map after the variable shift, with green lines for new connections and red strikethroughs for the ones that broke. And beside them all, a printout of his peer review notes, covered in his partner's handwriting.

"I barely recognize Draft 1," he said.

Emma pulled up a chair. She looked at the four drafts without touching them. "What's different?"

"Everything. Draft 1 has, what, twelve connections? And most of them are obvious first-order stuff. 'Bank replaces loan officers, employees lose jobs.' Draft 3 has feedback loops I didn't even know existed a week ago."

"Give me one."

James traced a line on Draft 3. "Cost savings from automation reduce branch staffing, which reduces the bank's presence in unbanked communities, which pushes customers toward informal lenders, which increases default rates in the broader economy, which makes regulators tighten lending rules, which increases the bank's compliance costs, which eats into the savings that started the whole cycle." He sat back. "That loop isn't anywhere in Draft 1. I didn't see it until the variable shift exercise forced me to think about the unbanked population."

"So the variable shift taught you to see it."

"Right. But here's the weird part." James pointed at Draft 2, the merged map. "Claude actually mentioned informal lenders in its analysis. I marked it with a C for attribution. But I didn't connect it to the regulatory feedback loop until I had to revise my own map under pressure. The information was sitting right there in Draft 2, and I didn't do anything with it."

Emma leaned forward. "That's the difference between information and understanding. You had the data point. You didn't have the mechanism."

James nodded slowly. "So the AI gave me breadth. The variable shift gave me depth. And my partner during the peer review..." He flipped to the handwritten notes. "She found a gap I still haven't fixed. She asked why I didn't connect employee morale to customer experience through service quality. I had the employee domain and the customer domain sitting right next to each other, and I never drew the line."

"Why not?"

"Honestly? I think I was so focused on the big dramatic loops, the regulation loop, the market competition loop, that I missed the simple human one. Unhappy employees give worse service. That is the most basic feedback loop in business. I managed a team for six years. I should have seen it day one."

Emma almost smiled. "You managed a team for six years, and the obvious connection was invisible to you."

"Because I was looking for complexity. I thought systems thinking meant finding the hidden connections. Turns out some of the important ones aren't hidden at all. They're just ordinary."

## The Growth Map: Turning the Lens on Your Own Thinking

You have spent four exercises building, comparing, revising, and defending a systems analysis. You traced cascading consequences, identified feedback loops, adapted to changing conditions, and survived a peer cross-examination. Now you do one more thing: apply the same systems thinking to your own learning.

This is the **Self-Evaluation Feedback Loop**, and it works exactly like the feedback loops in your cascade map. Your actions (doing the exercises) produced effects (changes in your thinking). Those effects circled back to influence your later actions (how you approached the next exercise). The question is whether you can trace that loop with the same rigor you brought to the bank scenario or the hospital scenario.

Vague claims do not count. "I got better at systems thinking" is the equivalent of a cascade map with no feedback loops: it lists an effect without explaining the mechanism. The Growth Map requires evidence.

### What You Do

Gather all four artifacts from this chapter:

- **Draft 1**: Your original cascade map (Exercise 1, before AI)
- **Draft 2**: Your merged map with attribution (Exercise 2, after AI comparison)
- **Draft 3**: Your revised map after the variable shift (Exercise 3)
- **Peer feedback**: Your partner's critique and your defence notes (Exercise 4)

If you also saved your AI Check responses from each exercise, include those. They contain specific feedback about what you missed and where your reasoning was strong.

Now build a Growth Map using the format below.

### The Thinking Trajectory

For each of the three categories, trace your growth across all four drafts. Do not skip to comparing Draft 1 and Draft 3 only. The path matters.

**Category 1: Feedback Loop Quality**

| Draft             | What Your Feedback Loops Looked Like                                                              | What Changed and Why                                |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |

| Draft 2           | (After seeing AI's analysis, did you add or improve feedback loops? Which ones?)                  | (What did the AI comparison teach you about loops?) |
| Draft 3           | (After the variable shift, did your loops get more sophisticated? Did new ones emerge?)           | (What did the variable shift force you to see?)     |
| After Peer Review | (Did your partner challenge any of your loops? Were they right?)                                  | (What did human critique reveal that AI missed?)    |

**Category 2: Cross-Domain Connections**

| Draft             | How Many Domains Connected                                                | What Changed and Why                                        |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Draft 1           | (Did you connect domains to each other, or treat them as separate lists?) | (Starting point)                                            |
| Draft 2           | (Did AI reveal cross-domain connections you missed?)                      | (What type of connections does AI see that you did not?)    |
| Draft 3           | (Did the variable shift create new cross-domain effects?)                 | (Which domains became connected that were separate before?) |
| After Peer Review | (Did your partner find missing cross-domain links?)                       | (What does this reveal about your mental model?)            |

**Category 3: Blind Spots**

| Draft   | What You Missed                                                             | Pattern                                                                                   |
| ------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Draft 1 | (What did AI find in Exercise 2 that was completely absent from your map?)  | (Type of thinking you skipped: breadth? cultural effects? regulatory consequences?)       |

James stared at his own Thinking Trajectory. The third row of the Blind Spots category bothered him.

"It's the same blind spot," he said. "Every time."

Emma waited.

"Human factors. Employee morale, customer trust, the personal relationship stuff. In Draft 1, I missed the loan officer-community trust connection. In Draft 2, I had it because Claude mentioned it, but I marked it as 'C' for Claude's contribution: I didn't generate it myself. In Draft 3, when the variable shift introduced the unbanked population, I had to build new human-factor connections from scratch, and I struggled. Then my partner found the employee morale gap in the peer review." He pushed his chair back. "Four exercises. Same blind spot. Four times."

"Why?"

James thought about it. He had managed a team. He dealt with human factors every day in his old job. "I think... when I'm doing analysis, I switch into a different mode. I look for structural patterns, economic incentives, regulatory triggers. The human stuff feels too obvious to write down. Like, of course unhappy employees give worse service. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows it. You didn't map it."

"Because I thought it was too simple."

"That's the **Obvious Connection Trap**." Emma pulled a napkin toward her and drew two circles labeled "Obvious" and "Mapped." The circles barely overlapped. "What everyone knows and what everyone maps are almost entirely separate sets. The gap between them is where most real-world systems actually break."

James looked at the napkin diagram. "So the feedback loop in my own thinking is: I assume obvious things don't need to be written down, so I skip them. Then when conditions change, the skipped connections are exactly the ones that matter most, and my analysis breaks. The variable shift proved that."

"Now you have a name for it. And a mechanism."

"And a way to fix it." James picked up a pen. "Before I call any map done, I go through each domain and ask: what's the most boring, obvious connection I haven't drawn? The one I'd be embarrassed to include because it feels too simple?"

Emma nodded. "That is a thinking protocol. Not a vague intention to 'do better.' A specific action triggered by a specific condition."

## The Self-Evaluation Feedback Loop

What James did in that conversation is the core skill of this exercise. He did not just say "I improved." He traced a specific blind spot across four drafts, identified the mechanism that kept it hidden, gave it a name (the Obvious Connection Trap), and designed a corrective protocol.

That sequence has a name: the **Self-Evaluation Feedback Loop**. It has four steps:

1. **Evidence**: Compare drafts side by side. Point to specific connections, loops, or gaps. No claims without artifacts.
2. **Pattern**: Look across exercises for recurring themes. One missed connection is an accident. The same type of missed connection appearing three times is a pattern.
3. **Mechanism**: Explain why the pattern persists. What about your background, your thinking habits, or your assumptions keeps this blind spot alive?
4. **Protocol**: Design a specific corrective action. Not "try harder" or "be more careful." A concrete step: "Before submitting, scan each domain for the most obvious connection I haven't drawn."

The Self-Evaluation Feedback Loop is itself a feedback loop. Your evidence changes your pattern recognition, which changes your mechanism understanding, which changes your protocol, which changes how you approach the next systems thinking task, which produces new evidence. The loop does not end with this chapter.

### Why "I Got Better" Is Not Enough

Compare these two self-evaluations:

**Vague**: "My systems thinking improved. My later maps are more detailed and have more feedback loops."

**Specific**: "In Draft 1, I treated each domain as a separate list. My 'feedback loops' were actually just two-step cause-and-effect chains: 'employees lose jobs, morale drops.' By Draft 3, I had genuine loops with four or five steps that circled back to the starting point. The variable shift exercise caused this change because it forced me to trace what happened when a connection I assumed was stable turned out to depend on a variable I had taken for granted."

The first version tells you nothing actionable. The second version identifies the exact exercise that caused the shift and explains the mechanism. A student who writes the second version will carry that improvement forward. A student who writes the first version will start the next chapter at the same level they started this one.

James had been quiet for a minute, reading through his peer review notes. "There's something else."

"Go on."

"My partner challenged my regulation feedback loop. She said the timeline was wrong: that regulators don't respond that quickly to market changes. I defended it during the session. I said something about emergency measures and fast-tracked policy." He paused. "But I think she was right. I think I built a feedback loop that sounds logical but wouldn't actually happen at the speed my map implied."

Emma said nothing. She was waiting to see if he would finish the thought himself.

"The loop was correct in structure but wrong in timing. And timing matters in a real system. A feedback loop that takes ten years to complete has a completely different practical impact than one that completes in six months."

"Is that in any of the AI feedback you received?"

James checked. "No. Claude rated my regulation loop as 'logically sound.' ChatGPT didn't flag the timing either." He looked up. "My partner caught something that three rounds of AI feedback missed."

"Why?"

"Because she's a policy analyst. She knows how regulators actually work. AI knows how regulators are described in training data. Those aren't the same thing."

Emma picked up the napkin again and added a third circle: "Lived Experience." It overlapped with "Obvious" but not with "Mapped."

"Three kinds of knowledge," she said. "What's formally modeled. What's obvious to everyone. And what you only know because you've done the work. Your partner had lived experience with regulatory timelines. AI had neither lived experience nor the sense that something obvious was missing. You had the business experience to see the human-factor gaps but not the policy experience to see the timing problem."

James pulled his Growth Map closer and added a line to the Blind Spots category: "Assumed all feedback loops operate on similar timescales. Need to add timing estimates to loops."

> **Trigger**: [When this condition occurs...]
> **Action**: [I will do this specific thing...]
> **Because**: [This blind spot persists because...]

**Action**: Scan each domain for the most obvious, boring connection I haven't drawn. The one that feels too simple to include.
**Because**: I consistently skip human-factor connections (morale, trust, service quality) because they feel too obvious to map, but they are often the connections that break first when conditions change.
:::

## Try With AI

Use these prompts in your preferred AI assistant. Each one helps you extract more insight from the work you have already done.

### Prompt 1: The Draft Comparison

```
I am evaluating my own growth in systems thinking across four
exercises. Here is my original cascade map (Draft 1) and my final
revised map (Draft 3).

Draft 1:
[paste your Draft 1]

Draft 3:
[paste your Draft 3]

Compare these two maps and identify:
(1) The 3 most significant improvements from Draft 1 to Draft 3
(2) For each improvement, which exercise likely caused the change
(3) Any thinking patterns from Draft 1 that still appear in Draft 3
    (blind spots I haven't fixed)
(4) One strength in Draft 1 that I may have LOST in Draft 3
    (overcorrection)
```

**What you are learning**: AI can spot differences between two artifacts faster than you can, but it cannot tell you WHY you changed. When you compare the AI's structural analysis against your own explanation of the mechanism, you learn whether you understand your own growth or are just noticing surface-level changes.

### Prompt 2: The Feedback Audit

```
Here is all the feedback I received on my systems thinking this
chapter: AI feedback from three exercises, and peer feedback from
a cross-examination.

AI feedback (Exercise 1): [paste]
AI feedback (Exercise 2): [paste]
AI feedback (Exercise 3): [paste]
Peer feedback (Exercise 4): [paste]

Analyze this feedback as a dataset:
(1) What themes appear across multiple feedback sources?
(2) Which feedback was UNIQUE to my peer (not found in AI feedback)?
(3) Is there feedback I received early that I seem to have addressed
    by later exercises?
(4) Is there feedback I received multiple times that I never
    addressed? What does that pattern suggest about my blind spots?
```

**What you are learning**: Treating feedback as data rather than individual comments reveals patterns. A blind spot flagged once is a data point. The same blind spot flagged across three exercises is a pattern that needs a protocol, not just awareness.

### Prompt 3: The Protocol Stress Test

```
I have designed a Growth Protocol to address my systems thinking
blind spots. Please stress-test it:

My most persistent blind spot: [describe it]
My protocol:
  Trigger: [when this happens]
  Action: [I will do this]
  Because: [this is why the blind spot persists]

Challenge my protocol:
(1) Is the trigger specific enough that I will actually remember it?
(2) Is the action concrete enough to execute, or is it vague?
(3) Does the "because" explanation actually match the blind spot, or
    am I misdiagnosing the root cause?
(4) Can you think of a scenario where my protocol would fail?
(5) Suggest one modification to make the protocol more robust.
```

**What you are learning**: A protocol you cannot stress-test is a wish. AI can simulate conditions where your corrective action might fail, helping you refine it before you encounter a real systems thinking challenge in a later chapter.

### What This Teaches You

The most important feedback loop in this chapter is not the one in your cascade map. It is the one in your own thinking. Every exercise produced data about how you reason: what you saw, what you missed, what changed after feedback, and what stubbornly persisted. The Self-Evaluation Feedback Loop teaches you to treat that data with the same rigor you brought to analyzing a bank or a hospital. If you can trace the mechanism of your own improvement with specific evidence, you will carry that improvement into every future chapter. If you can only say "I got better," you probably did not.
