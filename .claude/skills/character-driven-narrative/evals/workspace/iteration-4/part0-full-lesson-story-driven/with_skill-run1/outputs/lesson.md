# The Feedback Loop

James had never been someone who kept old drafts. At his operations job, the final version was the only version. You revised the supplier report, you sent it, you moved on. Nobody went back to compare the Tuesday draft with the Friday draft to see how their analysis had changed.

But now he had three drafts spread across his desk, and Emma had told him not to throw any of them away.

Draft 1 sat on the left. He had drawn it the first night of the chapter, alone at the kitchen table, tracing lines from a bank's decision to replace human tellers with AI chatbots. Five stakeholder domains. Each one had two or three arrows flowing outward from the decision. It looked clean. Organized. The kind of one-page summary he would have pinned to a project board at work.

The merged map sat in the middle, crowded with annotations in three colors: black for his original connections, blue for the ones Claude had found, red for the ones ChatGPT had surfaced that neither he nor Claude caught. It looked chaotic. A map that had survived a collision.

Draft 3 sat on the right. The post-variable-shift version. Half the original connections crossed out, new ones drawn in green ink where the changed context had forced him to rebuild.

"They don't look like the same project," James said.

Emma sat across from him, her coffee already half gone. She picked up Draft 1 and held it flat like she was reading a blueprint. "Tell me about this one."

"That's the first one. Five domains. Pretty straightforward."

"Straightforward how?"

James leaned in. "Every arrow goes one direction. Decision hits employees, employees lose jobs. Decision hits customers, customers lose personal service. Decision hits competitors, competitors see an opening." He paused. "It's a list, basically. A list wearing a disguise."

"What disguise?"

"Arrows. I drew arrows between boxes so it would look like a system, but it's really just a bullet-point list of consequences. Nothing connects to anything else. Nothing feeds back."

Emma set Draft 1 down and picked up Draft 3. She did not say anything. She just held it out toward him.

James took it. "This one's different. The arrows loop. Customer churn reduces revenue, which changes how much the bank actually saves from the chatbot switch, which changes whether they expand the program or pull it back. The regulator responds to public complaints, which depend on how the transition gets handled, which the bank adjusts based on the regulatory pressure." He traced the circle with his finger. "It's the same bank, the same decision, but Draft 3 treats everything as connected. Draft 1 treats everything as separate."

"Did you learn new facts between Draft 1 and Draft 3?"

He thought about it. "No. The scenario was the same. I mean, the variable shift changed the customer demographics, but the banking industry facts didn't change. I had all the same information. I just... did something different with it."

"That's where the interesting question is," Emma said.

## What Actually Changed

James picked up a pen and pulled a blank sheet from the stack. He wanted to make a list of improvements, the kind of end-of-quarter self-review he used to write at work. Things done better. Metrics improved. Check the boxes.

"I'll just go through each draft and note what got better," he said.

"Before you do that," Emma said, "I want you to try something. Pick one difference between Draft 1 and Draft 3. Any difference. Tell me about it."

"Okay." James looked at the two maps. "In Draft 1, I missed the regulatory feedback loop entirely. In Draft 3, I have regulators responding to public sentiment, adjusting oversight based on how the bank handles the transition, and the bank adjusting its rollout speed based on regulatory pressure. It's a whole loop I didn't have before."

"Good. Now: if I gave you a completely different scenario tomorrow, say a hospital replacing experienced nurses with AI triage systems, would catching a regulatory loop help you?"

"Sure. Health care is heavily regulated. I'd look for the same kind of pattern."

"What if the scenario was a city replacing bus drivers with autonomous vehicles?"

"Same thing. Transport regulators, public safety boards..."

"So that specific improvement, finding regulatory feedback loops, would transfer to any scenario you encounter?"

"Probably, yeah."

"Now pick a different change between Draft 1 and Draft 3."

James scanned the maps again. "In Draft 3, I added a connection between employee morale and customer experience. When tellers get replaced, the remaining employees feel insecure, their service quality drops, and that affects customer satisfaction even for customers who never interact with the chatbot."

"Would that transfer to the hospital scenario?"

James opened his mouth, then closed it. "Maybe? Nurse morale affecting patient care... actually, yes. But it's not the same kind of insight. The regulatory one was about my process: I learned to look for institutional actors. The employee-morale one was about this specific scenario. I noticed something about banking that could apply elsewhere, but the reason I noticed it was that my peer pointed it out during the cross-examination. I didn't develop a new habit. I just got a tip."

Emma leaned back in her chair. "You just made the most important distinction in self-evaluation."

## The Thinking-Change Test

"Here's what you're separating," Emma said. "Content changes and thinking changes."

James frowned. "Is that an official thing, or something you just made up?"

"Does it matter? Try the definition on for size. A content change is when your output gets better because you added something you were missing. You did not have the regulatory loop; now you do. A thinking change is when your process gets better. You used to stop tracing effects when you ran out of obvious ideas. Now you stop when the causal chain circles back. The first one improves one map. The second one improves every map you will ever make."

"Okay, but..." James put the pen down. "Aren't content changes worth something? I caught a real gap. The regulatory loop was important."

"They're worth a lot. For this map. What they're not worth is compounding interest."

He stared at her.

"A content change pays once," Emma said. "A thinking change pays every time you sit down to analyze something. If all you did this chapter was collect six new connections you missed, you improved one deliverable. If what you did was change _how you approach finding connections_, you improved the system that produces deliverables."

"That's like..." James paused, reaching for it. "At my old job, we had a supplier who kept delivering late. My boss wanted me to call them every Thursday to remind them. That fixed the problem for that supplier. But when I restructured the PO system so that every supplier got automatic reminders three days before delivery, the problem disappeared across the whole vendor base. The call was a content fix. The system change was a process fix."

Emma blinked. "I've been explaining this concept to people for years and nobody has put it that way."

"Really?"

"The supplier analogy is better than what I was using. Keep going with it."

James wrote on his blank sheet: **Thinking-change vs. content-change. Content = calling one supplier. Thinking = fixing the PO system.**

This is what the **Thinking-Change Test** separates. When you compare your early work to your late work, the question is not "did my output improve?" The question is "did my _process_ improve?" A content change improves one artifact. A thinking change improves the system that produces all future artifacts.

:::tip The Thinking-Change Test
Compare your Draft 1 and Draft 3 side by side. For every difference you spot, ask: "If I faced a completely different scenario tomorrow, would this improvement still help me?" If yes, you found a thinking change. If it only helps with this specific scenario, it is a content change. Both have value, but thinking changes compound.
:::

## Where Did the Growth Come From?

Emma stood up and walked to the kitchen counter to refill her coffee. "You have evidence from four exercises. Don't waste it."

"What do you mean, evidence?"

"You have Draft 1, which is your baseline. You have the AI comparison from Exercise 2, which showed you gaps in breadth. You have the variable shift from Exercise 3, which broke connections you were confident about. You have peer feedback from Exercise 4, which exposed places where your understanding was shallower than your map suggested." She turned back with her coffee. "That's four different mirrors. Each one showed you a different thing about your thinking."

"They felt different, too," James said. "The AI comparison was... informative. Like reading someone else's version of the same report. I could see what they included that I missed. But it didn't sting."

"And the variable shift?"

"The variable shift stung." James looked at his Draft 3. Three of his feedback loops had collapsed when the customer demographics changed from urban tech-literate customers to 60% unbanked. His "customers switch to competitors" chain fell apart because unbanked customers don't have alternatives. His "social media backlash" loop weakened because unbanked populations often have less online presence. "I had to admit that connections I thought were universal were actually built on assumptions I hadn't even noticed."

"What assumptions?"

"That the customers had smartphones. That they had bank accounts with competitors. That they were active on social media. I never wrote those down anywhere, but every connection I drew depended on them." He shook his head. "The AI comparison showed me things I missed. The variable shift showed me things I had wrong. Those are not the same."

"And the peer defence?"

James thought about his Exercise 4 experience. His partner had challenged his feedback loop between employee morale and service quality. The challenge was simple: "How do you know that remaining employees feel insecure? Could they feel relieved that the chatbots handle the boring work?" He had argued back, but the argument was weaker than he expected. He could draw the connection but he couldn't explain the mechanism convincingly.

"The peer defence showed me the difference between drawing a connection and understanding it," James said slowly. "I could draw the arrow, but when someone pushed on it, I realized I was reasoning from assumption, not from evidence."

"So. Three different mirrors. Three different types of growth."

James started writing. Not a list this time. A table.

| Feedback Source                     | What It Showed Me                                               | Type of Growth                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI comparison (Exercise 2)          | Connections and domains I missed entirely                       | **Additive**: I learned to cast a wider net                                        |
| Variable shift (Exercise 3)         | Assumptions hidden inside connections I was confident about     | **Corrective**: I learned to test my own certainty                                 |
| Peer cross-examination (Exercise 4) | Places where my understanding was thinner than my map suggested | **Depth**: I learned the difference between mapping something and understanding it |

This is **Growth Source Mapping**: the practice of tracing each thinking change back to the specific feedback source that produced it. Different sources reveal different weaknesses. Additive growth (from the AI comparison) comes from exposure to perspectives you lacked. Corrective growth (from the variable shift) comes from having your assumptions tested under changed conditions. Depth growth (from peer defence) comes from being forced to explain, not just depict.

Each type of growth requires different practice to sustain. Additive growth improves with wider reading, more perspectives, more diverse input. Corrective growth improves with stress-testing, deliberately changing your own assumptions and seeing what breaks. Depth growth improves with explaining your reasoning aloud to someone who will push back.

## Building the Improvement Map

Emma checked the time on her phone. "I need to call someone. Twenty minutes." She picked up her jacket from the chair. "When I get back, I want to see your improvement map."

"What's an improvement map?"

"Not a list of mistakes. Not a diary entry about what you learned." She was halfway to the door. "A map. Something with a direction." She left.

James stared at the blank paper.

He had written end-of-project reviews before. They always went the same way: what went well, what didn't, what we'd do differently. The templates had checkboxes. You filled them in, filed them, and nobody read them again. The reviews changed nothing because they were too vague to act on. "Improve communication with stakeholders" appeared on every single one.

He did not want to write that kind of document.

He started with the evidence. Three drafts. Four exercises. Feedback from AI, from the variable shift, from his peer. He had more data on his own thinking process than he had ever collected on anything at his old job.

**Section 1: What Changed (Thinking Changes Only)**

He applied the Thinking-Change Test to each difference he could identify.

_Change 1: My stopping rule._ In Draft 1, he stopped tracing effects when he ran out of obvious things to write. Each domain got two or three consequences, and then he moved to the next domain. In Draft 3, he stopped when the causal chain looped back to something already on the map, or when he could articulate why the chain terminated. The difference: exhaustion-based stopping vs. closure-based stopping. This would transfer to any system he analyzed.

_Change 2: My assumption awareness._ Before the variable shift, he treated every connection as universal. "Customers switch to competitors" felt like a fact. After the variable shift broke three of his connections by changing one demographic parameter, he started flagging which connections depended on unstated conditions. He began writing conditional notes next to arrows: "if tech-literate," "if urban." This was a thinking change. He had moved from treating connections as facts to treating them as hypotheses with conditions.

_Change 3: My explanation depth._ Before the peer defence, he was satisfied with connections he could draw. After his peer exposed a gap in his employee-morale argument, he started asking himself "could someone argue the opposite?" before committing to a connection. Not every connection survived that test. The ones that did were stronger. The ones that didn't got revised.

**Section 2: What Should Have Changed But Didn't**

James went back through the AI feedback from all four exercises. He looked for the pattern that appeared more than once.

It was there. In Exercise 1, the AI had flagged that he underweighted institutional actors: regulators, industry bodies, oversight boards. In Exercise 2, the AI comparison showed that both Claude and ChatGPT had found institutional-actor connections he missed. In Exercise 4, his peer had questioned why his map treated regulation as a one-time event rather than an ongoing relationship.

Three exercises. Three signals. Same blind spot.

He wrote: _I consistently treat institutional actors as static constraints instead of dynamic participants. Regulators set rules and I draw them as walls. But regulators respond to what the system does, just like every other actor. My operations background trained me to treat compliance as a checkbox: you meet the requirement and move on. In systems thinking, compliance is a living relationship that changes based on what every other actor does._

This blind spot had not fully resolved by Draft 3. He had added some regulatory feedback loops after the AI comparison pointed them out, but when the variable shift changed conditions, his first instinct was still to adjust customer and employee connections. The regulatory loops were the last thing he updated. They were an afterthought, not a reflex.

**Section 3: What to Change Next**

James wrote a single concrete practice: _On my next cascade map, I will start with institutional actors. Not customers, not employees, not competitors. I will put regulators, oversight bodies, and industry groups in the center of the map for the first pass and trace their feedback loops before I draw anything else. After that first pass, I will add the stakeholders I normally start with, and I will check whether the institutional-actor loops change any of the connections I would have drawn by default. If they do, that confirms my blind spot is real and this practice helps. If they don't, I will look for a different blind spot._

He put the pen down. The improvement map had taken twelve minutes. It fit on one page. But every line pointed at specific evidence from specific exercises, and the practice at the bottom could be tested in thirty minutes with any new scenario.

The front door opened. Emma came back, hung her jacket, and picked up the sheet without sitting down. She read it standing.

"Your stopping rule shifted," she said. "From exhaustion to closure."

"First thing I wrote down."

"And the institutional-actor blind spot. How many exercises flagged it?"

"Three out of four."

She set the page down and tapped the third section. "This is the part that matters. Your practice is testable. You will know within one exercise whether starting with institutional actors changes the loops you find."

"That's what I was going for."

"Most people write 'get better at systems thinking' and consider themselves done."

James looked at the improvement map. "That's what I would have written at my old job. 'Improve stakeholder analysis.' File it. Forget it."

"Why is this different?"

"Because 'improve stakeholder analysis' can't fail. It's too vague. You can always convince yourself you improved a little. But 'start with institutional actors and check whether it changes your feedback loops' has a concrete outcome. Either the loops change or they don't. Either I find connections I would have missed or I don't."

"And if it doesn't work?"

James paused. He hadn't thought about that. "Then... my blind spot isn't really about institutional actors. It's about something else. And I have to look at the evidence again and figure out what."

"Which is another feedback loop."

James looked at the page. Then at Draft 1. Then at Draft 3. Then back at the improvement map.

"The whole chapter is a feedback loop," he said.

Emma sat down across from him. She picked up Draft 1 and placed it on the left. Placed the improvement map on the right. Drew an invisible line between them with her finger.

"Draft 1 was your input. Four exercises were your process: amplifying loops, dampening loops, variable shifts, peer pressure. Each one changed something about how you approach the work. This improvement map is your output." She paused. "And what happens to the output?"

"It becomes the input for the next chapter."

"The same structure you mapped for the bank's customers and regulators."

James sat with that for a moment. The cascade maps he had drawn across four exercises used a particular structure: identify the actors, trace the connections, find where the output of one actor feeds back into the input of another. He had been doing that with banking scenarios and health care scenarios and transportation scenarios. And now he was doing it with himself. His early thinking was the input. The exercises were the process. His improved thinking was the output. And the improvement map fed that output back into how he would approach the next system.

"It's the same pattern," he said. "Applied to me."

"It usually is."

This is the **Personal Improvement Map**: a self-evaluation tool that transforms backward reflection into forward practice by treating your own learning as a system with inputs, processes, outputs, and feedback loops.

### How to Build Your Improvement Map

| Section                      | Question to Answer                                                                                                                                     | Evidence to Use                                                        |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **What changed**             | Which thinking habits shifted between Draft 1 and Draft 3? (Apply the Thinking-Change Test: only count changes that would transfer to a new scenario.) | Side-by-side comparison of drafts, your change logs from each exercise |
| **What should have changed** | What pattern appears in the feedback you received across multiple exercises? (Look for the same weakness flagged by different sources.)                | AI evaluations, peer feedback, variable shift results                  |
| **What to change next**      | What is one specific, testable practice you will try in your next analysis? (It must be concrete enough to fail.)                                      | The weakest pattern identified in section 2                            |

The test for a good improvement map: can it fail? If your stated practice is specific enough that you could look at your next piece of work and determine whether the practice succeeded, it is a real map. If it is too vague to produce a clear success-or-failure verdict, it is a wish wearing a plan's clothing.

## Try With AI

Use these prompts in your preferred AI assistant to deepen your self-evaluation.

**Prompt 1: The Blind Spot Detector**

> Here is my Draft 1 cascade map: [paste Draft 1]. Here is my Draft 3 cascade map: [paste Draft 3]. Compare them and identify three differences. For each difference, tell me whether it is a content change (I added something I was missing) or a thinking change (my analytical approach shifted). Explain your reasoning for each classification.

_What you are learning:_ You are training yourself to distinguish between improved output and improved process. The AI acts as a second evaluator for your self-assessment, which helps you calibrate whether you are correctly identifying thinking changes or accidentally counting content changes as process improvements.

**Prompt 2: The Hidden Assumption Finder**

> Here are three connections from my cascade map that I am most confident about: [list three connections]. For each one, identify at least two assumptions I am making that, if changed, would break or reverse the connection. Be specific about what would need to change and why the connection would fail.

_What you are learning:_ You are practicing corrective self-evaluation. The variable shift in Exercise 3 broke connections by changing one parameter. This prompt teaches you to run that test proactively, finding the hidden conditions your confident connections depend on before an external force exposes them.

**Prompt 3: The Improvement Map Stress Test**

> Here is my Personal Improvement Map: [paste all three sections]. Evaluate it against these criteria: (1) Are my thinking changes genuinely about process, not just content? (2) Is my recurring-pattern claim supported by evidence from multiple exercises, or could I be overgeneralizing from one data point? (3) Is my improvement practice specific enough that I could test it in thirty minutes with a new scenario? Give me a revised version of any section that does not meet its criterion.

_What you are learning:_ You are using AI as a quality check on your self-evaluation. The AI cannot know whether your improvement plan will work, but it can identify whether the plan is stated precisely enough to be testable. Vague plans survive every review. Specific plans get challenged, and that challenge makes them better.

## PRIMM-AI+ Practice

### Predict

Before looking at your evidence, write down your prediction: what do you think was your single biggest thinking change across this chapter? Keep it to one sentence. Set it aside.

### Run

Lay out Draft 1, Draft 3, all AI feedback, and your peer notes side by side. Work through the three sections of the Personal Improvement Map. Write each section before moving to the next.

### Investigate

Now retrieve your prediction from the first step. Compare it to what the evidence actually showed. Did you predict the right thinking change, or did the evidence point somewhere you did not expect? If they diverge, that divergence itself is a data point about your self-awareness: you know less about your own thinking habits than you assumed.

### Modify

If your predicted change and your evidence-based change differ, add a note to your improvement map: "My self-model predicted X, but the evidence showed Y. Next time I self-evaluate, I should weight [evidence type] more heavily than my intuition."

### Make

Apply your improvement map to one quick exercise: draw a 5-minute cascade map for any scenario (a school replacing textbooks with tablets, a restaurant replacing waiters with ordering apps). Then check: did you start with institutional actors? Did you flag conditional assumptions? Did you apply your new stopping rule? Score yourself honestly on each practice you committed to.

### What This Teaches You

The feedback loop structure you mapped for banks, hospitals, and cities applies to your own learning. Self-evaluation is not a soft exercise in "reflection." It is a systems thinking skill with the same architecture you have been studying all chapter: identify inputs (your early work), trace the process (what feedback shifted your approach), evaluate the output (your improved work), and design the next iteration (your improvement map). The students who build this habit of treating their own learning as a system with traceable feedback loops will improve faster across every chapter that follows, because they are not just learning content. They are tuning the process that learns.

You have now completed the **Systems Thinking Portfolio**: five exercises that took you from a first-draft cascade map to a personal improvement plan. Bring it with you.
