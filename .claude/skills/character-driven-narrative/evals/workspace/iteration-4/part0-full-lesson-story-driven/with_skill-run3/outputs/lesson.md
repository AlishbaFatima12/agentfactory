# The Feedback Loop

James spread three pieces of paper across the table. Draft 1 on the left, marked with his original arrows and circled domains. Draft 3 on the right, twice as dense, with feedback loops drawn in red. In the middle, a blank page.

"What's the blank one for?" Emma asked, setting her coffee down across from him.

"I figured I should write something about what I learned." He stared at the blank page. "But I don't know where to start. The maps look different. I can see that. I just can't explain why."

Emma picked up Draft 1 and held it at arm's length. "How many feedback loops?"

James leaned over. "Two. No, one and a half. That second one doesn't actually loop back."

"And Draft 3?"

"Five. Real ones."

"So something changed in your head between these two maps." She set Draft 1 down. "The question is: what changed, and what caused it to change?"

## Exercise 5: The Feedback Loop (Self-Evaluation)

**Layers Used:** Layer 6 (Iterative Drafts), Layer 1 (Reflection)

:::note Building On the Full Chapter
This exercise requires your **Draft 1** (from Exercise 1), your **Draft 3** (from Exercise 3), your **peer feedback** (from Exercise 4), and all **AI feedback** you received across the chapter. Gather these before starting.
:::

### What You Do

Place your Draft 1 and Draft 3 side by side. For every difference between them, identify what changed and what caused the change: AI feedback, peer feedback, the variable shift exercise, or your own reflection. Then look across all your changes for a pattern in what you tend to miss. That pattern is your **Blind Spot Profile**: the most valuable output of this entire chapter.

---

James picked up a pen. "Alright. The obvious one: Draft 1 has nothing about regulators. Draft 3 has an entire branch showing how public backlash triggers regulatory hearings, which triggers compliance costs, which loops back to reduce the original cost savings."

"Where did the regulator branch come from?"

He thought about it. "Claude. When I compared my map to Claude's in Exercise 2, the first thing I noticed was that Claude had a whole regulatory chain I'd missed."

"Good. That's **Source Attribution**: knowing which input improved which part of your thinking." Emma tapped the pen against Draft 1. "Now. Did you add that regulatory chain because Claude told you to, or because you understood _why_ it belonged?"

James opened his mouth, then closed it.

"Different question," Emma said. "If I gave you a new scenario right now, no AI, would you include regulators?"

"Yes," James said. "Automatically. It's not something I'd have to remember. After seeing how the regulatory feedback loop basically ate the cost savings in my scenario, I can't look at any big decision without asking who regulates it."

"That's the difference between copying a correction and **internalizing an insight**. One lives in your notes. The other lives in your thinking."

---

### Delta Analysis: What Changed and Why

**Delta Analysis** is the practice of comparing two versions of your own work to identify what improved, what degraded, and what stayed the same. It is not about counting: "Draft 3 has more arrows" is not analysis. It is about understanding the mechanism of each change.

For every difference between Draft 1 and Draft 3, record three things:

1. **What changed**: Describe the specific addition, removal, or restructuring
2. **Source**: What caused this change? (AI feedback, peer feedback, variable shift, self-discovery)
3. **Depth test**: Is this a surface change (added an effect you were told to add) or a structural change (reorganized how you think about a domain)?

James worked through his map for ten minutes while Emma checked something on her laptop. He had a growing list:

_Regulator branch: added entire domain. Source: AI (Claude). Depth: structural. I now check for regulatory effects automatically._

_Employee morale loop: strengthened mechanism. Source: peer. My partner asked "why would morale drop if nobody gets fired?" and I realized my original arrow had no mechanism. I rewrote it: remaining employees see AI replacing colleagues, fear they're next, productivity drops from anxiety._

_Customer trust: reversed direction. Source: variable shift. When I changed the timeline from "immediate replacement" to "gradual transition," the customer trust effect flipped from negative to cautiously positive. That taught me the speed of change matters as much as the change itself._

He stared at the fourth entry and frowned.

_Competitor response: added. Source: ... me?_

"Emma."

She looked up.

"There's a change I can't trace. In Draft 1, I didn't have anything about competitors copying the bank's AI strategy. In Draft 3, I do. But I don't remember any AI or peer telling me to add it."

Emma almost smiled. "When did you add it?"

"During the variable shift, I think. I was redrawing the map with the new timeline and I just... saw it. If one bank does it slowly and it works, every other bank follows. That creates an industry-wide shift, which changes the regulatory calculus completely."

"You connected two domains on your own. No prompt." She paused. "That's the change that matters most. AI can hand you a missing branch. A peer can challenge a weak mechanism. But when you start seeing connections unprompted, that's your systems thinking muscle actually working."

James wrote: _Source: self-discovery. Depth: structural. Emerged from redrawing, not from feedback._

---

### The Surface vs. Structural Test

Not all improvements are equal. Adding an effect because AI told you it was missing is useful, but it is a **surface improvement**: your map got better while your thinking stayed the same. Restructuring how you connect domains because you understood a new principle is a **structural improvement**: your map got better because your thinking got better.

Here is a test to tell the difference:

| Question                                                              | Surface Change | Structural Change               |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Could you reproduce this improvement on a new scenario without notes? | Probably not   | Yes, automatically              |
| Did the change affect only one branch or the whole map?               | One branch     | Rippled across multiple domains |
| Would you have made this change before the chapter started?           | No             | No, but now it feels obvious    |

"I have a problem," James said.

"Go on."

"Most of my changes are surface. Seven out of ten. I added things because AI told me to or my partner pointed them out. Only three feel structural."

"Is that a problem?"

"I mean... isn't the goal to have all structural changes?"

Emma shook her head. "The goal is to be honest about the ratio. Seven surface changes means you got better information. Three structural changes means you got better at thinking. Both matter. But if you claimed all ten were structural, you'd be lying to yourself."

She leaned forward. "Here's what I've learned after years of reviewing my own code. The ratio of surface to structural improvement is always lopsided. You always get more corrections than insights. But the insights compound. Those three structural changes will show up in everything you analyze from now on. The seven surface changes are one-time fixes."

James looked at her. "You've tracked this in your own work?"

"After a major project retrospective went badly. I thought I'd improved in dozens of ways. My tech lead asked me to sort them into 'things someone told you' versus 'things you figured out.' The honest list was embarrassing. But it was the first time I actually knew where I needed to grow."

### Source Attribution Map

Create a simple tally of your changes by source. This is your **Source Attribution Map**: a record of where your improvements came from.

| Source                      | Number of Changes | Example                                            |
| --------------------------- | :---------------: | -------------------------------------------------- |
| AI feedback (Exercise 2)    |      \_\_\_       | _e.g., Added regulatory branch_                    |
| Peer feedback (Exercise 4)  |      \_\_\_       | _e.g., Strengthened employee morale mechanism_     |
| Variable shift (Exercise 3) |      \_\_\_       | _e.g., Reversed customer trust direction_          |
| Self-discovery              |      \_\_\_       | _e.g., Connected competitor and regulator domains_ |

"My breakdown is four AI, two peer, one variable shift, three self-discovery," James said. "What does that mean?"

"What do you think it means?"

He studied the numbers. "That AI is my biggest source of new connections. It finds stuff I miss."

"And?"

"And I only have two from peer review, but one of them was the most useful change on the list. My partner asking 'why would morale drop?' forced me to actually explain my mechanism instead of just drawing an arrow."

"Different reviewers find different things," Emma said. "AI is broad: it catches missing domains, missing effects, things you forgot to consider. Peers are deep: they challenge the logic of what you already have. Neither replaces the other."

"And the self-discovery ones?"

"Those are the ones that tell you where you are. If your self-discovery count is zero, you're still relying entirely on external feedback. If it's growing across chapters, your systems thinking is becoming independent."

### The Blind Spot Profile

James pushed the source attribution aside and pulled Draft 1 back. "Okay, so I know what changed and why. But you said there's a pattern."

"Look at everything you missed in Draft 1. Not one at a time. All at once."

He listed his Draft 1 gaps:

- Missing: regulators entirely
- Missing: competitor response
- Weak: employee morale mechanism (arrow with no explanation)
- Missing: feedback loop between customer churn and revenue
- Weak: treated internal knowledge as static, not dynamic

"What do the missing ones have in common?"

James stared at the list. "They're all... outside the company. Regulators, competitors, the feedback loop that goes through customers and comes back. I got the internal stuff: employees, knowledge base, operations. But everything that left the building and came back, I missed."

"That's your **Blind Spot Profile**. You think inside-out. You start from the organization and trace effects outward, but you lose the thread once those effects hit external actors and bounce back."

"Is that bad?"

"It's a pattern. Everyone has one. Mine is temporal: I map spatial relationships well, but I underestimate how long feedback loops take to develop." She paused. "I once designed a caching system that worked perfectly for the current load. Six months later it collapsed because I hadn't traced how growing traffic would interact with the cache invalidation pattern. I mapped the space but not the time."

James wrote in his notebook: _My blind spot: external-to-internal feedback. I trace effects outward well but lose them when they bounce back from external actors. Strategy: after drawing any cascade map, explicitly ask "what comes back?" for every external domain._

"The strategy part matters," Emma said. "A blind spot you've named but can't compensate for is just self-awareness. A blind spot with a strategy is a thinking tool."

---

### Your Blind Spot Profile

Your **Blind Spot Profile** is the pattern in the types of connections you consistently miss. Everyone has one. Identifying it is the most valuable output of this chapter because it tells you exactly what to practice.

To find yours:

1. List every connection that was missing from Draft 1 but present in Draft 3
2. List every mechanism that was weak in Draft 1 but strengthened in Draft 3
3. Look for a category: Are your gaps spatial (missing entire domains)? Temporal (missing delayed effects)? Logical (weak mechanisms)? External (missing how effects return from outside)?
4. Write a one-sentence description of your pattern
5. Write a one-sentence strategy for catching it earlier

"I need to check something," Emma said, standing up. "Do your blind spot analysis. Write the pattern and the strategy. I'll be back in fifteen minutes."

James nodded. She picked up her coffee and walked toward the hallway.

Alone with his maps, James laid them side by side again. The pattern was obvious now that Emma had named it. Every gap on his Draft 1 followed the same shape: an effect left the organization, hit an external actor, and the external actor did something that circled back. He had traced the outward journey each time but stopped at the boundary.

He wrote: _Blind Spot Profile: I stop at the organizational boundary. Effects that leave and return are invisible to me until someone points them out._

Then the strategy: _After completing any systems map, do a boundary audit. For every external domain, ask: "What does this actor do next, and does any of that reach back to the original decision?"_

He tested it. New scenario, made up on the spot: a university replaces all teaching assistants with AI tutors. He traced the obvious effects: workload, student experience, cost. Then he ran his boundary audit. What do students do next? Some transfer to universities that still have human TAs. What do competing universities do? They advertise their human touch. What do accreditation bodies do? They investigate whether AI tutoring meets standards. Each of those bounced back: enrollment drops, marketing pressure, compliance costs.

Three feedback loops he would have missed an hour ago.

When Emma came back, James had a second map sketched on the back of his blank page.

"What's that?"

"I tested my strategy on a new scenario. University replacing TAs with AI." He pointed. "Three external feedback loops. All found using the boundary audit."

Emma studied it for a moment. "The accreditation loop is good. Most people miss that one." She pointed at the student transfer branch. "This mechanism is weak, though. You're assuming students transfer immediately. What's the realistic timeline?"

"Semesters, not weeks. So the feedback loop is slow."

"Which means?"

"The university might not notice the enrollment effect until it's too late to reverse course. The delay IS the danger." He grabbed his pen and annotated the loop: _slow feedback, 2-4 semester lag, may appear after decision is locked._

"Now you're thinking in systems," Emma said.

### Try With AI

After completing your self-evaluation, use these prompts to deepen your analysis.

**Prompt 1: Validate your blind spot**

```
I completed a systems thinking chapter where I drew cascade maps
across multiple exercises. My blind spot pattern is: [paste your
one-sentence pattern]. Here are the connections I missed in my
first draft: [list them].

Does my blind spot diagnosis seem accurate, or am I mis-categorizing
my gaps? Are there other patterns in my missed connections that I
might not be seeing?
```

**What you are learning:** AI can see patterns in your self-assessment that you might miss. If it identifies a different pattern than you did, that mismatch itself is informative: your blind spot may be deeper than you thought.

**Prompt 2: Stress-test your strategy**

```
My compensating strategy for my blind spot is: [paste your strategy].
Give me a new scenario (a major organizational decision) and walk me
through applying my strategy to it step by step. Then evaluate: did
my strategy catch the connections I would have missed, or does it
need refinement?
```

**What you are learning:** A strategy that works on your familiar scenario might fail on an unfamiliar one. This prompt tests whether your strategy is general enough to transfer.

**Prompt 3: Compare your growth to a benchmark**

```
Here is my Draft 1 cascade map: [paste Draft 1].
Here is my Draft 3 cascade map: [paste Draft 3].

Evaluate the growth between these two maps on four dimensions:
(1) Breadth: how many domains and connections were added
(2) Depth: how many feedback loops and higher-order effects improved
(3) Precision: how much did mechanism explanations improve
(4) Independence: what proportion of improvements came from my own
    insight vs. external feedback

Rate each dimension from 1-5 and explain what the rating means for
my next steps.
```

**What you are learning:** Structured evaluation frameworks give you a vocabulary for tracking your own growth over time. The four dimensions (breadth, depth, precision, independence) become a reusable self-assessment tool for any future analysis task.

### What This Teaches You

The exercises in this chapter followed a deliberate arc. You drew a map alone (Exercise 1), compared it with AI (Exercise 2), adapted it when conditions changed (Exercise 3), and defended it under peer questioning (Exercise 4). Each step improved your map. But improvement is only half the lesson.

The other half is knowing _how_ you improved. Delta Analysis gives you a method for comparing any two versions of your work. Source Attribution tells you which types of feedback help you most. The Surface vs. Structural Test keeps you honest about whether your work got better or just bigger. And the Blind Spot Profile gives you a permanent thinking tool: a named weakness with a named countermeasure.

Systems thinking is not something you learn once. It is something you calibrate continuously. The feedback loop in the title is not just the loops in your cascade map. It is the loop between doing the work, evaluating the work, finding the pattern, and doing the next round better.
