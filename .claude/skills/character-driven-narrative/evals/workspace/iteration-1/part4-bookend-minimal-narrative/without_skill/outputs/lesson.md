James stared at the third repeated block of logging-and-retry code he'd written that week and finally said it out loud. "I keep wrapping the same boilerplate around every function. There has to be a way to factor this out." Emma glanced over from her monitor. "There is. You're about to fall in love with decorators." He'd seen the `@` syntax before, mostly with `@staticmethod` and route handlers in Flask, but he'd never written his own. "I get that they exist," he said. "What I don't get is how a function can take a function and return a better function without the caller knowing anything changed." Emma raised an eyebrow. "That's a pretty good one-sentence summary, actually. The trick is that once you see the pattern, you'll start noticing it everywhere."

---

James pushed his chair back and looked at the decorator he'd just built: a clean `@retry` that wrapped any flaky API call with exponential backoff, and a `@validate_schema` that checked input shapes before the function body ever ran. Two decorators, maybe twenty lines each, replacing hundreds of lines of scattered try/except blocks across the codebase. "I keep thinking about where else this applies," he said. "Caching. Auth checks. Timing." Emma nodded. "Now you know why metaprogramming gets people excited. Just watch the instinct to over-abstract: if you're writing a decorator that only one function uses, you've probably gone too far." He bookmarked that advice. The real test would be the refactor he'd been putting off, a data pipeline with identical error handling in nine places. Nine copies of the same logic, about to become nine `@` signs and one clean definition.
