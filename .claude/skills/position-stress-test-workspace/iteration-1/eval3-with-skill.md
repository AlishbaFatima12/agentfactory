# POSITION STRESS TEST

---

## POSITION STATEMENT

We should lay off 15% of staff now to extend our cash runway sufficiently to reach profitability or our next funding milestone without requiring emergency capital.

---

## ARGUMENTS

**ARGUMENT 1:**
- **Claim:** A 15% reduction extends runway past the critical survival threshold, giving the company time to execute on revenue targets.
- **Evidence/Reasoning:** Payroll + benefits typically represent 60-80% of a startup/growth-stage company's burn. A 15% headcount reduction translates to roughly 9-12% burn reduction (accounting for severance costs, retained benefits, and non-linear savings). If current runway is ~9 months, this could push it to ~12-14 months — enough to hit the next inflection point without dilutive emergency funding.

**ARGUMENT 2:**
- **Claim:** A single decisive cut is less damaging to culture and execution than serial small cuts or a slow hiring freeze that creates ambiguity.
- **Evidence/Reasoning:** Research on organizational layoffs (e.g., Wayne Cascio's longitudinal studies, HBR analysis of downsizing outcomes) consistently shows that companies doing one clean cut recover morale and productivity faster than those doing "death by a thousand cuts." Multiple rounds of layoffs destroy trust because employees never believe it's the last one. A clear, one-time 15% reduction with transparent communication preserves the remaining team's ability to execute.

**ARGUMENT 3:**
- **Claim:** The alternative — running out of cash or raising at a severe down round — would harm all employees more than a targeted reduction harms 15%.
- **Evidence/Reasoning:** If the company runs out of runway, 100% of employees lose their jobs, equity becomes worthless, and the company's mission dies. A down round at desperation terms could dilute existing employees' equity by 70-90%, effectively wiping out their upside while still potentially requiring layoffs later. The 15% cut is the least-bad option in a set of bad options.

---

## CONFIDENCE: 72%

**Justification:** The financial logic is sound — burn reduction extends runway, and the math works directionally. But 72% rather than 90%+ because: (a) the 15% number may not be precisely right (could be too shallow or too deep), (b) execution risk is high (wrong people cut, key functions gutted), and (c) there's genuine uncertainty about whether the extended runway is *enough* to reach the milestone. The decision is defensible but not certain.

---

## REVERSAL TRIGGER

Any ONE of these would reverse this recommendation:

1. A credible term sheet arrives within 30 days at ≤20% dilution that provides 18+ months of runway
2. Financial remodeling shows that a 15% cut only extends runway by ≤3 months (meaning the cut is painful but insufficient)
3. Analysis reveals that the 15% cut would eliminate a function critical to the revenue milestone (e.g., the entire sales team needed to close the pipeline that justifies the runway extension)

---

## ADVERSARIAL DEFENCE (3 ROUNDS)

### ROUND 1 — SURFACE ATTACKS

**Counter-argument 1: "15% is an arbitrary number — you're cutting to a budget, not to a strategy."**

You haven't shown that the roles being eliminated are the *right* roles. If you start with "we need to cut 15%" rather than "here's the team we need to execute our revised strategy," you're optimizing for a spreadsheet, not for the business. Companies that cut to a number rather than to a plan often find they cut the wrong people and have to re-hire within 6 months — spending more than they saved.

**Counter-argument 2: "Your 'one clean cut' argument assumes you'll get it right the first time — but you almost certainly won't."**

The research you cite about single cuts working better assumes the cut is *sufficient*. But you're at 72% confidence, and you haven't stress-tested whether the runway extension actually reaches the milestone. If the market shifts, a key customer churns, or the product timeline slips by even one quarter, you'll be back doing Round 2 of cuts — which is exactly the "death by a thousand cuts" scenario you claimed to be avoiding. Your own uncertainty undermines your strongest cultural argument.

**Counter-argument 3: "You're treating employees as a cost line, not as the revenue engine."**

Every person cut is capacity removed. If your path to profitability requires building and selling, you're cutting the people who build and sell. The "extend runway" framing assumes the remaining 85% can generate the same output — but they can't. Productivity drops 20-40% post-layoff (Cascio's own data shows this). You may extend runway by 4 months on paper while actually *reducing* your ability to hit the revenue milestone by more than 4 months.

**Defence:**

1. The 15% figure should indeed be derived from strategy, not from arithmetic. The defence holds *only if* the cut is paired with a revised operating plan that identifies which functions are essential to the milestone and which are not. If the 15% is truly "cut the bottom performers across all teams" — that's indefensible. If it's "we're killing Product Line B and the teams that support it" — that's strategic. **Concession: the recommendation must be "cut 15% according to a revised strategic plan," not just "cut 15%."**

2. The risk of insufficiency is real. The defence is that a single well-calibrated cut *plus* a contingency plan (e.g., "if we miss Q3 revenue by >20%, we execute Plan B which is X") is still better than inaction or serial cuts. But the counter-argument correctly identifies that 72% confidence means ~1-in-4 chance this isn't enough. **Concession: the board presentation must include a scenario where the cut isn't sufficient, with a pre-committed decision tree for that case.**

3. The productivity argument is the strongest attack. The mitigation is: (a) generous severance and outplacement to reduce survivor guilt, (b) immediate role clarity for remaining staff, (c) explicit acknowledgment that Q1 post-layoff will be a recovery quarter, not a sprint quarter. The 20-40% productivity dip is real but temporary (typically 1-2 quarters). The math must account for this dip in the runway model. **Concession: the financial model must include a productivity-adjusted revenue forecast, not a linear extrapolation.**

**Position: Shifted.**
The position holds directionally, but the recommendation must now include: a strategy-driven cut plan (not a number-driven one), a contingency decision tree, and a productivity-adjusted financial model.

---

### ROUND 2 — STRUCTURAL ATTACKS

**Counter-argument 4: "Your concessions just made the layoff harder to execute — and you still haven't addressed whether leadership should cut itself."**

You've now added three conditions to the recommendation (strategic plan, contingency tree, productivity-adjusted model). Each adds weeks of preparation. Meanwhile, you're burning cash. If the situation is urgent enough to warrant a 15% cut, it's too urgent for a multi-week planning exercise. And notably absent from your stakeholder matrix: the executive team's own skin in the game. Are leaders taking pay cuts? Are any executives being eliminated? If the answer is "we're cutting 15% of staff but 0% of leadership," the cultural argument collapses — the remaining 85% will see this as leadership preserving itself at employees' expense.

**Counter-argument 5: "Your Argument 3 (alternatives are worse) is a false trilemma — you've only compared layoff vs. bankruptcy vs. down round."**

Where is the option of aggressive revenue acceleration with the current team? Pivoting to a higher-margin product line? Negotiating extended payment terms with vendors? Converting some salaries to equity? A temporary 20% pay cut across the board (including leadership) that preserves all jobs? Bridge financing from existing investors? You've presented three options when there are at least eight. This is a classic anchoring bias — you anchored on the layoff and then compared it only to catastrophic alternatives to make it look reasonable.

**Counter-argument 6: "Your reversal trigger requires a term sheet within 30 days — but have you actually pursued one?"**

If you haven't exhausted fundraising options, recommending layoffs first is premature. The reversal trigger is meaningless if no one is testing it. Is there an active fundraise? Have existing investors been asked for a bridge? Has the board explored strategic partnerships? If the answer to any of these is "no," then you're recommending the most painful option without having tested the less painful ones. That's not decisive leadership — it's avoidance of the harder work of fundraising.

**Defence:**

4. The leadership accountability attack is devastating and correct. **Full concession: the recommendation must include executive compensation reductions (minimum 20-30% for C-suite, board fee waivers) executed simultaneously with or before the layoff.** Without this, the cultural argument is not just weak — it's actively harmful. On the preparation timeline: the strategic planning can be compressed to 1-2 weeks with dedicated effort. The alternative (cutting blindly and fast) is worse than 2 weeks of additional burn.

5. The false trilemma attack has partial merit. Some alternatives (temporary pay cuts, vendor renegotiation, bridge financing) should genuinely be explored or ruled out *before* recommending layoffs. However: temporary pay cuts rarely generate sufficient savings (a 20% cut across 100 people saves less than eliminating 15 roles with full-loaded costs), they create retention risk among top performers who have options, and they don't actually reduce headcount-driven costs like benefits and infrastructure. Vendor renegotiation is marginal. Revenue acceleration with the current team assumes the current team is the bottleneck — but if the problem is market timing or product-market fit, more effort from the same team won't fix it. **Concession: the board presentation must explicitly list all alternatives considered and why each was rejected, with actual numbers — not just "we considered other options."**

6. The fundraising attack is valid if and only if fundraising hasn't been pursued. **If there is no active fundraise or bridge conversation, the recommendation changes to: "Pursue bridge financing for 30 days while preparing the contingency layoff plan. Execute the layoff only if financing fails."** If fundraising has been exhausted, this counter-argument falls away. **Concession: the recommendation must document the fundraising status and whether a parallel path (raise + prepare cuts) is feasible.**

**Position: Shifted further.**
The layoff may still be the right answer, but it cannot be the *first* answer. The recommendation must now be sequenced: (1) pursue bridge/alternatives with a hard 30-day deadline, (2) prepare the strategic cut plan in parallel, (3) execute the cut only if alternatives fail. And it must include leadership sacrifice.

---

### ROUND 3 — FOUNDATION ATTACKS

**Counter-argument 7: "The fundamental assumption underlying this entire position is that the company *deserves* to survive — but does it?"**

You're treating company survival as an axiom. But if the company needs to lay off 15% of staff to stay alive, something went wrong. Was it a bad business model? Poor capital allocation? Over-hiring during a boom? Leadership misjudgment about market timing? The people you're proposing to fire didn't cause this problem — they were hired by the same leaders who are now recommending their elimination. Before asking "how do we survive?", the board should ask: "Is this company's thesis still valid?" If the thesis is broken, extending runway via layoffs just delays an inevitable death while causing real harm to real people in the interim. You could be trading 15% of people's livelihoods to buy 4 months of leadership denial.

**Counter-argument 8: "You've framed this as a rational financial decision, but it's actually a values question you're avoiding."**

Your entire analysis is in spreadsheet language: burn rate, runway, milestones, dilution. But the actual question is: what obligation does a company have to the people it hired? You recruited these people. You asked them to leave other jobs, maybe relocate, maybe turn down other offers. Some joined because of the mission you sold them. Now you're telling 15% of them that the promises were contingent on financial performance they had no control over. The framework you're using (financial optimization) is designed to make this feel like math. But it's not math. It's a moral decision about how much suffering you're willing to impose and on whom. By keeping it in financial language, you're avoiding the ethical weight.

**Counter-argument 9: "The strongest case against this layoff is the one you'll never hear — from the people who get cut."**

Your stakeholder analysis lists laid-off employees, but you'll never actually hear their defence. They won't be in the board meeting. They won't see the financial model. They'll get a 30-minute meeting, a severance package, and a door. The entire process is designed so that the people most affected have the least voice. If the 15% who are cut could present their own case — their projects, their contributions, their alternatives, their family situations — would the decision survive? The fact that the process structurally silences the most-affected stakeholders should make you uncomfortable about the quality of the decision.

**Defence:**

7. This is the most important question and the hardest to answer. The company's thesis *must* be re-validated before executing layoffs. If the market has moved, if the product doesn't work, if the unit economics are fundamentally broken — then layoffs are indeed just buying time on a sinking ship. **The defence is: layoffs are defensible ONLY if the post-cut plan has a credible path to sustainability.** "Extend runway and hope" is not a plan. "Extend runway to close Enterprise Customer X which is in late-stage negotiation and would make us cash-flow positive" *is* a plan. **Concession: the board presentation must include a brutally honest assessment of whether the thesis still holds, and if it doesn't, the recommendation should be an orderly wind-down, not a layoff.**

8. This attack is philosophically correct and practically unavoidable. The moral weight is real and should not be hidden behind financial language. **The defence is not that this isn't a moral decision — it is — but that the moral calculus includes the 85% whose jobs and equity are preserved, the customers who depend on the product, and the mission that attracted everyone in the first place.** The moral argument *against* layoffs (obligation to those hired) must be weighed against the moral argument *for* them (obligation to everyone else). Neither side is clean. **Concession: the board presentation should name this as a moral decision, not just a financial one. The CEO should personally communicate with every affected employee, not delegate to HR.**

9. This is structurally true and there is no complete defence. The process *is* asymmetric. The mitigation — generous severance, outplacement support, extended benefits, accelerated vesting, genuine references — is real but insufficient to fully address the power imbalance. **The best partial defence is process fairness: clear criteria for who is cut (not political, not arbitrary), applied consistently, reviewed by someone outside the decision chain (board member or external advisor), with a genuine appeal mechanism even if rarely used.** The person being cut should be able to understand *why them specifically* and believe the answer is honest.

**Position: Held — but fundamentally reframed.**
The recommendation survives, but it is no longer "lay off 15% to extend runway." It is now a conditional, sequenced, morally-acknowledged decision with extensive prerequisites.

## REFLECTION

**Hardest counter-argument:** Counter-argument 7 — "Does this company deserve to survive?" This was hardest because it challenges the unstated axiom of the entire analysis. Every other argument operates within the frame of "company should survive, question is how." This one steps outside the frame and asks whether the premise itself is valid. If the thesis is broken, every downstream argument about runway and milestones is optimization of a doomed outcome. It forces genuine intellectual honesty about whether leadership is recommending layoffs to save the company or to delay confronting their own failure.

**Did the position change?** Yes — substantially in form, though not in ultimate direction. The original position ("lay off 15%") transformed into a conditional, sequenced, multi-prerequisite recommendation that barely resembles the original. The *direction* held (headcount reduction is likely necessary), but the *shape* of the recommendation changed completely.

**What does this reveal about the structure of the reasoning?** The original position was under-specified and over-confident. It treated a deeply complex, multi-stakeholder, morally weighted decision as a financial optimization problem. The adversarial process revealed that the financial case was necessary but nowhere near sufficient — the decision requires strategic justification, exhaustion of alternatives, leadership sacrifice, thesis re-validation, moral acknowledgment, and process fairness. A board that receives only the financial case should be suspicious. A board that receives all of this might actually be equipped to decide.

**Final confidence: 65%** (was 72%)

Confidence dropped because the adversarial process revealed the decision's validity depends on prerequisites that haven't yet been confirmed (thesis validity, fundraising exhaustion, strategic cut plan). The financial logic is sound, but the decision is only as good as its prerequisites.

## WHAT TO BRING TO THE BOARD

If you proceed with this recommendation, the board presentation should include:

1. **Thesis re-validation** — Is the business model still viable? Be brutally honest.
2. **Alternatives exhausted** — Bridge financing, pay cuts, vendor renegotiation, revenue acceleration — each with actual numbers showing why rejected.
3. **Strategic cut plan** — Which functions stay, which go, and why. Not a percentage applied evenly.
4. **Leadership sacrifice** — Executive pay cuts, board fee waivers, executed before or simultaneously with layoffs.
5. **Productivity-adjusted financial model** — Assume 20-30% productivity dip for 1-2 quarters post-cut.
6. **Contingency decision tree** — If the cut isn't enough, what's Plan B? Pre-commit now, not later.
7. **Moral framing** — Name it as what it is. Don't hide behind "restructuring" language.
8. **Process fairness** — Clear criteria, independent review, generous severance, personal communication by CEO.
