# Summary: Contract Analysis: Obligation Extraction

## What This Lesson Covers

Most organisations know they have vendor contracts. Few know what those contracts actually commit them to. This lesson teaches structured contract analysis, extracting every obligation, SLA, and key date from vendor agreements using the `/contract` command, then systematically identifying risk clauses before they cause operational or financial harm.

## The Core Problem

Contracts are signed and filed. The notice period for preventing auto-renewal is buried on page 17. The liability cap limits recovery to three months of fees. The price escalation clause compounds at 5% annually. None of this is tracked. The first anyone knows is when the auto-renewal fires, the claim is rejected above the cap, or the budget is wrong.

## The Four Contract Analysis Task Types

The `/contract` command supports four workflows: **obligation extraction** (structured tables of what each party must do, by when, with what consequence), **risk flagging** (identifying the six categories of unfavourable clauses), **contract summary** (plain-language one-pager for non-legal readers), and **renewal strategy input** (current terms plus performance data plus negotiation levers).

## Obligation Extraction Output

A complete extraction produces five sections: Our Obligations (what the organisation must do), Vendor Obligations (including every SLA with its target and breach consequence), Key Dates (every deadline with required notice period and action), SLA Table (metrics, measurement, reporting, and credits), and Auto-Renewal Flags (notice deadline, method, and action required).

The Key Dates table is the most operationally critical section, not because of the renewal date, but because of the notice deadline. The notice deadline is the last date the organisation can act before inaction becomes a binding commitment.

## The Six Risk Flag Categories

The six flags most frequently causing unintended liability are: auto-renewal traps, liability caps, price escalation clauses, unilateral change rights, termination-for-convenience asymmetry, and absence of data ownership and return clauses. SLAs without defined consequences are also flagged: a target without a remedy is aspirational, not contractual.

## Negotiation Positions

For each flag, the analysis produces a five-component negotiation position: issue, desired position, rationale (market standard or business logic), acceptable alternative, and walk-away threshold. Defining the walk-away condition before negotiation starts is what creates leverage, without it, negotiations drift toward accepting whatever the vendor offers.
