### Core Concept

Islamic fintech is scaling faster than standard-setters can respond — digital murabaha, robo-advisers, P2P Islamic lending, and impact sukuk all operate in production while the accounting frameworks were written for brick-and-mortar banks — the practitioner's challenge is reasoning from first principles under existing IFRS and AAOIFI rather than applying rules specifically designed for these products.

### Key Mental Models

- **Clear vs Interpretive vs Open**: Every fintech accounting question falls into one of three categories: clear (IFRS 15 applies straightforwardly to robo-advisory fees), interpretive (IFRS 9 SPPI test for impact sukuk requires judgment about cash flow characteristics), or genuinely open (FCA authorisation category for Islamic P2P platforms was never designed for this structure). Distinguishing these three categories is the professional skill.
- **Platform vs Bank Revenue Split**: When a fintech platform acts as a bank's agent (wakeel), the platform earns a wakala fee recognised under IFRS 15, while the bank earns murabaha income recognised under IFRS 9 — the same transaction creates different revenue streams with different recognition standards for different parties.

### Critical Patterns

- Digital murabaha must be tested against the murabaha/tawarruq distinction: if the customer never intends to take physical delivery of the underlying asset (e.g., gold), the transaction may be commodity murabaha (tawarruq), which some Shariah authorities prohibit
- For P2P Islamic lending, the foundational question is who qualifies as the Islamic Financial Institution — the platform, each individual saver, or no one — and the answer determines which regulatory framework applies
- Impact sukuk structure choice (ijarah vs musharaka) directly affects investors' IFRS 9 classification and whether the SPPI test passes
- Regulatory frameworks across Malaysia (BNM sandbox), UK (FCA categories), and UAE (ADGM) were not designed for Islamic fintech — practitioners must navigate genuine regulatory gaps

### Common Mistakes

- Assuming fintech products need entirely new accounting frameworks — every structure can be analysed under existing IFRS and AAOIFI principles by reasoning from first principles
- Treating all digital murabaha as genuine murabaha without testing whether the asset purchase has real economic substance or is merely a tawarruq funding mechanism
