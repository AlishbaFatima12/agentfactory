### Core Concept

Nigeria is Africa's most active sovereign sukuk issuer using ijarah structures backed by road infrastructure, and the most transferable lesson is the contractor independence principle: Islamic finance structures affect only parties to the Shariah contract — a road contractor's IFRS 15 accounting is identical whether the government finances via sukuk or conventional bonds.

### Key Mental Models

- **Contractor Independence Principle**: Only parties to the Islamic finance contract adjust their accounting for the Shariah structure. Contractors, suppliers, landlords, and service providers outside the Shariah contract apply standard IFRS without Islamic modification — ask "Is this party a signatory to the Shariah contract?" to determine scope.
- **Sovereign Sukuk Derecognition**: The FGN's purchase undertaking at face value means risks and rewards of road assets are not transferred to the SPV, so derecognition fails — assets stay on the government's balance sheet and sukuk proceeds are a financial liability.

### Critical Patterns

- CBN Non-Interest Banking Framework mandates IFRS for financial reporting and AAOIFI Shariah Standards for product structuring — Jaiz Bank is the reference case
- ECL staging for sovereign sukuk reflects actual sovereign credit risk: Nigeria's sub-investment grade rating produces measurable (not negligible) Stage 1 provisions, unlike AAA-rated sovereigns
- Africa's infrastructure financing gap ($100 billion annually) drives sovereign sukuk issuance across Nigeria, Senegal, Ivory Coast, South Africa, and Egypt
- IFRS 9 SPPI test: FGN ijarah sukuk with fixed distributions and purchase undertaking passes SPPI, supporting amortised cost classification

### Common Mistakes

- Assuming the sukuk structure affects all parties in the ecosystem — it does not; the contractor applies IFRS 15 identically regardless of how the government raises funds
- Treating sovereign sukuk as zero credit risk — Nigerian sovereign credit risk is real and requires meaningful ECL provisioning
