# Meeting Prep and Vendor Management — Summary

## Core Concept

This lesson teaches structured meeting preparation as a professional discipline — replacing ad-hoc "review my notes five minutes before" with a systematic workflow that produces briefings, surfaces vendor obligations, and captures post-meeting action items. Using the meeting-briefing skill and `/vendor-check`, students learn to walk into any meeting (vendor negotiation, board update, regulatory discussion) with structured talking points, red lines, walk-away positions, and a clear view of what the other party already owes. The lesson demonstrates that preparation quality directly determines meeting efficiency and negotiation outcomes.

## Key Mental Models

- **5-Step Meeting Preparation Methodology**: Identify context, assess preparation needs, gather information, synthesise into structured briefing, identify gaps — this sequence ensures systematic coverage rather than ad-hoc recall.
- **Meeting Type Determines Template**: The same meeting-briefing skill produces fundamentally different outputs for vendor negotiations (talking points, red lines, walk-away positions) versus board meetings (RAG risk status, compliance posture, budget variance) — the audience and purpose shape the structure.
- **Vendor Obligation Dashboard (30/60/90-Day View)**: A time-horizon approach to vendor management that surfaces upcoming deadlines, overdue items, and SLA compliance in one view, turning scattered contract obligations into actionable intelligence.
- **Cross-Referencing Briefing with Obligations**: The meeting briefing tells you what to discuss; the vendor dashboard tells you what the vendor already owes — combining both produces negotiation leverage that neither provides alone.
- **Priority-Based Follow-Up Cadence**: HIGH items get daily check-ins, MEDIUM get weekly, LOW get monthly or dependency-triggered — follow-up frequency must match the consequence of missing the deadline.
- **Preparation Gaps as Explicit Output**: Identifying what the briefing _could not find_ (unknown attendee names, missing email history, unreviewd templates) is as valuable as what it did find — gaps become pre-meeting action items.

## Critical Patterns

- Provide rich context in the meeting briefing prompt (current contract terms, what you want changed, your constraints, your role) — the skill produces better structured output when it understands your negotiation position.
- Always cross-reference the vendor obligation dashboard with the meeting agenda — SLA shortfalls and overdue deliverables strengthen your negotiation position on other items.
- Capture action items immediately after the meeting with priority, ownership, deadline, and follow-up cadence — reconstructing from memory hours later loses detail and accountability.
- Notice the structural difference between meeting types: vendor negotiations need red lines and walk-away positions; board meetings need executive summaries and RAG-coded risk highlights; regulatory discussions need compliance documentation and disclosure strategy.
- With connectors (calendar, email), the briefing pulls context automatically; without connectors, the same structured output is produced from manually provided context — the difference is data gathering effort, not output quality.

## Common Mistakes

- Walking into a meeting knowing what you want changed but not knowing your walk-away position — the briefing forces you to define the point at which you escalate or decline.
- Treating all action items with the same follow-up cadence — a DPA template due Friday needs daily tracking, while a follow-up meeting scheduled for next week needs no tracking until its dependencies resolve.
- Not checking the vendor's existing obligations before a renegotiation — missing that the SLA fell below guarantee or that a promised deliverable is overdue weakens your negotiation position.
- Overlooking the auto-renewal notice deadline — if renegotiation stalls, you need to know your hard deadline to give notice or default to existing terms.
- Preparing for a board meeting with the same template as a vendor negotiation — board members need risk posture and budget variance, not talking points and red lines.
